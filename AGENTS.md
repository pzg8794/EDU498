## Course Theme

All EDU498 work should follow Piter's Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS framework.

The course lens is not generic literacy. It is literacy as access, identity, survival, healing, joy, criticality, and transformation for students whose learning is shaped by intersecting identities and conditions.

Core identities and conditions to keep in view include:

- neurodivergence,
- culturally and linguistically diverse identity,
- Dominican / Latino identity,
- chronic illness,
- depression and mental health,
- trauma,
- disability and access needs,
- multilingual and transnational experience,
- multiple identities or overlapping conditions.

## Puzzle Plan Lens

The Puzzle Plan treats each assignment, reading, reflection, and placement example as one piece of a larger system for educational equity.

When writing or summarizing, connect the work to at least one of these dimensions:

1. **Access** — What barriers prevent participation, and what design choices reduce those barriers?
2. **Identity** — How are students' cultures, languages, bodies, histories, and lived experiences recognized?
3. **Healing** — How does the work avoid shame, deficit thinking, invisibility, or forced masking?
4. **Agency** — How do students gain voice, choice, authorship, or control over meaning-making?
5. **Criticality** — How does the work help students question power, bias, stereotypes, systems, and dominant narratives?
6. **Joy** — How does the work create belonging, curiosity, aesthetic fulfillment, confidence, or shared success?
7. **Transformation** — How does the work move differences from inconvenience, to assets, toward brilliance?

## EQUITAS Framework Lens

EQUITAS should be used as the equity architecture behind the course theme. In EDU498, EQUITAS means designing learning spaces where equity is not an afterthought but part of the structure.

Use EQUITAS to ask:

- Who is being treated as the default learner?
- Whose language, body, culture, pace, attention, health, or emotional state is being assumed?
- Which students are being labeled because the classroom design is too narrow?
- What forms of literacy are being recognized, and which are being ignored?
- What supports make meaningful participation possible?
- How can students show knowledge through multiple modes, not only traditional writing?
- How can instruction interrupt deficit narratives instead of reproducing them?

## Writing Process

Follow this process unless Piter explicitly asks for something different:

1. **Preserve labels while drafting.** Keep sections labeled until the writing is complete. Combine only after Piter asks to merge or polish.
2. **Do not make petty edits.** Avoid unnecessary wording changes. Focus on meaningful structure, argument, grammar problems that actually matter, and assignment alignment.
3. **Use surgical changes.** When Piter asks for surgical edits, provide minimal targeted changes, not full rewrites.
4. **Respect voice and rhythm.** Piter's writing often blends personal narrative, academic reflection, and systems analysis. Preserve that rhythm.
5. **Ground work in course materials.** Connect to readings, videos, assignments, and placement examples when relevant.
6. **Use the Puzzle Plan theme consistently.** Do not produce generic teaching reflections when the assignment can connect to access, neurodivergence, CLD identity, chronic illness, trauma, depression, or overlapping identities.
7. **Be explicit about uncertainty.** If readings, files, or prompts are not accessible, say so instead of inventing details.
8. **Verify before linking.** When a file is created or updated, verify it exists at the exact EDU498 path before giving the user the link.
