## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

**"Who reads what, in which formats, and why?"**  
This stood out because literacy is not only print. In my teaching practice, students read icons, interfaces, code blocks, social cues, images, AI outputs, and peer feedback. That matters especially for neurodivergent and CLD students, whose literacies may be present but not always recognized by school. It pushes me to ask what counts as reading in a classroom, and whose meaning-making gets overlooked when we only value traditional academic forms.

**"Mirrors, Windows, and Sliding Glass Doors"**  
This reminded me that students need texts and classroom experiences that reflect their identities, help them understand others, and invite them into new possibilities. For students with multiple identities, representation cannot be one-dimensional. A student should not have to choose which part of themselves gets seen; literacy should create space for culture, language, disability, interests, and imagination to exist together.

**"Remember the human."**  
This connects to digital literacy for me. When students use online tools, AI, or peer feedback, they need to learn privacy, authorship, care, and responsibility. Technology can make interaction feel distant or automatic, but every post, comment, prompt, and response still affects real people. For me, digital literacy has to include empathy and ethical judgment, not only procedural fluency.

---

## 2 Questions

1. How can teachers assess multimodal literacies without forcing every student to prove understanding only through print-based responses?
2. What would literacy assessment look like if it treated debugging, oral explanation, peer support, visual design, and revision as real evidence of understanding?

---

## 1 Connection to Teaching Practice

In my Pine Brook IGNITE placement and Minecraft unit work, I keep returning to the difference between an access barrier and a thinking barrier. A student might struggle with a click-path, written directions, or a new interface while still showing strong reasoning by explaining a choice, helping a peer debug, or revising after feedback. That pushes me to define literacy more broadly: students are already reading, composing, critiquing, and creating across many modes. My job is to design learning so those literacies become visible, especially for students whose identities and ways of communicating are too often misread.

Systems should adapt to human complexity, not force students into narrow categories.
