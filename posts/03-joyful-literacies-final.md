## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

**"Joy, love, and aesthetic fulfillment."**  
This stood out because joy is not just about making school more pleasant. Joy is connected to dignity, access, and the feeling that your mind is allowed to show up fully. In my teaching and research work, I keep returning to students whose strengths are misread because their language, neurotype, body, or way of communicating does not match the expected school pattern. For neurodivergent students, CLD students, and students with multiple identities, joyful literacy has to include multiple ways to read, compose, speak, build, revise, and be recognized.

**"Liberation, self-determination, self-reliance, and self-empowerment."**  
This phrase helped me think about literacy as agency rather than compliance. A literacy pursuit should not only ask students to complete a product; it should help them see themselves as designers, coders, speakers, writers, artists, and meaning-makers. That connects to my Pine Brook IGNITE placement and unit work, such as Minecraft, where students showed deep thinking through debugging, design choices, peer explanation, and revision. But here is the tension: those practices are only meaningful if the institution recognizes them as literacy. A student who debugs elegantly, explains a strategy to a peer, and revises a build after feedback is doing real literacy work, but if the only evidence that counts is a written paragraph, that student's agency disappears from the record. Liberation requires not just practice but recognition.

**"Shared with an authentic audience."**  
This reminded me that literacy becomes more meaningful when it matters to someone beyond the teacher. When students create only for a grade, the work can feel disconnected from their lives. But when they create for classmates, families, communities, or a public audience, literacy becomes social and purposeful. It gives students a reason to revise, explain, advocate, and care about how their message is received. For students who are often underestimated, an authentic audience can also become a way for their knowledge to be seen more fully and meaningfully.

## 2 Questions

1. How can teachers create joyful literacy experiences without making joy feel forced, especially for students whose experiences with school have included frustration, exclusion, medical or disability-related misunderstanding, or being treated through a deficit lens?
2. What would assessment look like if it valued joy, agency, collaboration, revision, oral explanation, debugging, design, and multimodal expression as real evidence of literacy growth?

---

## 1 Connection to Teaching Practice / Future Classroom

In my Pine Brook IGNITE placement, particularly in the Minecraft unit work, I have seen joy appear when students have structure, choice, and room to create. A student might struggle with written directions, a click-path, or a digital interface but still show strong thinking by debugging, explaining a design choice, helping a peer, or revising after feedback. That reminds me not to confuse an access barrier with a thinking barrier. In my future K–12 computer science classroom, joyful literacy means designing learning where students can use language, code, visuals, speech, collaboration, and technology to make meaning. For neurodivergent students, CLD students, and students with multiple identities, that kind of design can make their strengths visible while still keeping the work rigorous. Concretely, that means building in oral explanation as a legitimate assessment option, treating a student's debugging process as evidence of reasoning, and creating assignments where the audience is real: a classmate, community, or public showcase, not only a grade. Systems should not require students to become less complex in order to be understood, and neither should classrooms.

Planning with the end in mind reframes assessment from something we do to students at the finish line into something we design into the learning from the beginning — I have found myself referring back to this technique a lot when updating my lessons. This really matters for students whose strengths are not always visible through traditional print-based tasks. However, the tension I keep sitting with is: even when we plan backward with complex, multimodal assessments in mind, the institutional gradebook still often requires a single score. So, the next layer of this may be not just how we assess but what the system allows us to record as evidence of learning, something I am still navigating and understanding better through my teaching placement.
