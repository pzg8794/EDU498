## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

**"Cultural identities are affirmed, valued, and used as vehicles for teaching and learning."**  
This stood out because culture should not be treated as decoration or as something separate from academic work. In literacy and computer science, students bring cultural knowledge through language, interests, problem-solving habits, family experiences, technology use, and peer collaboration. For neurodivergent and CLD students, this matters because school often notices what does not fit before it notices the knowledge students are already using.

**"Identity should be understood as dynamic and multiple, rather than static and unitary."**  
This quote helped me think about students who live at the intersection of multiple identities. A student is never only "CLD," only "neurodivergent," only "a student with an IEP," or only "good/bad at literacy." Cultural pedagogy asks teachers to resist flattening students into one label and instead design classrooms where language, disability, culture, interests, and lived experience can be understood together.

**"Elevate historically marginalized voices" and "empower learners to be agents of positive social change."**  
This connects cultural pedagogy to critical literacy. It is not enough for students to see themselves in the curriculum; they also need opportunities to question systems, create meaning, and act with agency. In digital literacy, that includes asking who technologies serve, whose privacy is protected, whose language is recognized, and whose knowledge gets treated as legitimate.

---

## 2 Questions

1. How can teachers design culturally responsive literacy instruction without turning culture into a stereotype or asking students to perform identity for the classroom?
2. What would assessment look like if it valued translanguaging, oral explanation, visual design, peer support, debugging, and community knowledge as real evidence of literacy and thinking?

---

## 1 Connection to Teaching Practice

In my Pine Brook IGNITE placement and Minecraft unit work, I have seen how much students bring into digital literacy tasks before formal instruction even begins. Some students use gaming knowledge, peer language, visual reasoning, debugging strategies, or collaborative problem-solving to make sense of a task. A student may struggle with a written direction or interface sequence while still showing deep thinking through explanation, design choices, or helping a peer. Cultural pedagogy pushes me to treat those practices as resources, not side notes. In my future classroom, I want literacy instruction to make students' cultural, linguistic, neurodivergent, and technological ways of knowing visible while still holding them to rigorous and meaningful work.
