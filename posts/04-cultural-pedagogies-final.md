## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

**"Cultural identities are affirmed, valued, and used as vehicles for teaching and learning."**  
This stood out because culture should not be treated as decoration or something separate from academic work. Students bring culture into how they speak, question, collaborate, use technology, solve problems, and make meaning. In my placement reflections, I keep returning to the idea that a student's struggle with the school-facing version of a task does not mean the student lacks knowledge. For neurodivergent students, CLD students, and students with multiple identities, this matters because school often notices what does not fit before it notices the knowledge students are already using.

**"Identity should be understood as dynamic and multiple, rather than static and unitary."**  
This quote helped me think about students who, like me, live at the intersection of multiple identities. A student is never only "CLD," "neurodivergent," "a student with an IEP," or "good/bad at literacy." This connects to the way I have been thinking across my course work and future research: systems often simplify people in order to process them, but teaching should resist that flattening. Cultural pedagogy asks teachers to design classrooms where language, disability, culture, interests, family knowledge, technology use, and lived experience can be understood together.

**"Elevate historically marginalized voices."**  
This connects cultural pedagogy to critical literacy. It is not enough for students to see themselves in the curriculum; they also need opportunities to question systems, create meaning, and act with agency. In digital literacy and computer science, that means asking whose language is recognized, whose privacy is protected, whose data is used, whose knowledge is treated as legitimate, and who benefits from the tools students are asked to use. That question matters to me because my broader equity work is about systems that misread people and how we might design classrooms, assessments, and technologies that recognize human complexity more accurately.

## 2 Questions

1. How can teachers design culturally responsive literacy instruction without turning culture into a stereotype or asking students to perform identity for the classroom?
2. What would assessment look like if it valued translanguaging, oral explanation, visual design, peer support, debugging, and community knowledge as real evidence of literacy and thinking?

## 1 Connection to Teaching Practice / Future Classroom

In my Pine Brook IGNITE placement, one example that connects more directly here is the Grade 4 AI as a Thought Partner / AI Playground lesson. The goal was not just for students to "use AI," but for them to explore AI as a tool for brainstorming and problem-solving while also evaluating the output and explaining how they might use it for learning. That made the literacy work visible in several ways: students had to read an AI response, decide whether it made sense, revise their question or prompt, and explain a step, pattern, choice, or fix. The lesson planning also reminded me that cultural pedagogy depends on access: students needed chunked steps, visual modeling, clear success criteria, and flexible entry points so the technology did not hide their thinking. A student might struggle with setup, menu navigation, or the wording of a prompt while still showing strong reasoning through their explanation, revision, or peer support. In my future K–12 computer science classroom, I want literacy instruction to make students' cultural, linguistic, neurodivergent, and technological ways of knowing visible while still holding them to rigorous and meaningful work. Cultural pedagogy does not lower the bar; it widens what counts as clearing it.
