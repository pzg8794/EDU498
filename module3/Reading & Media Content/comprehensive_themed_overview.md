## 1. Overall Module Frame

Module 3 focuses on digital and media literacy. Through the course theme, this module is not only about learning how students use technology. It is about understanding digital literacy as access, identity work, critical participation, community responsibility, and self-protection.

The central question is:

> How can digital and media literacy help students who are often misread by school systems become more visible, more connected, more critical, and more fully themselves?

Digital literacy is not neutral. Students enter digital spaces with different levels of access, support, language background, cultural recognition, emotional safety, and prior experience. For this reason, digital citizenship and media literacy must be taught through equity, not only rules.

## 3. Source-by-Source Synthesis

### 1. Knobel & Lankshear

This source provides the conceptual foundation for new literacies. Literacy is not fixed, print-only, or limited to traditional school tasks. It changes as tools, technologies, communities, and social practices change.

Through the course theme, this source helps explain why students may show powerful literacy through digital communication, remixing, gaming, visual design, collaboration, and online participation even when traditional school measures miss those strengths.

### 2. Price-Dennis, Holmes, & Smith

This source places digital literacy inside an inclusive classroom. It shows that technology can support access when students are given multiple ways to create, explain, collaborate, represent understanding, and participate.

Through EQUITAS, the reading asks whether digital tools are actually reducing barriers or simply reproducing old tasks on screens.

### 3. Stornaiuolo & Thomas

This source frames youth digital activism as literacy practice. Students use digital tools to create public messages, organize around issues, challenge dominant narratives, and participate in civic life.

Through the Puzzle Plan, this reading connects to agency and criticality because students become authors, organizers, and meaning-makers rather than passive consumers.

### 4. Brooks & Frankel

This source challenges deficit perspectives by recognizing adolescents' transnational digital literacy practices. Students' multilingual, family, community, and cross-border digital practices are meaningful literacies.

Through EQUITAS, this reading asks teachers to stop treating monolingual, local, English-dominant literacy as the default measure of intelligence.

### 5. Serpagli & Mensah

This source connects social media practices to science learning in an all-girls classroom. It shows how familiar digital practices can support STEM identity, visual explanation, collaboration, and engagement.

Through the course theme, this reading helps expand who gets seen as a science learner and how science understanding can be represented.

### 6. Melo-Pfeifer & Gertz

This source focuses on disinformation about minorities and critical intercultural news literacy. It teaches students to examine representation, missing context, media framing, and the assumptions behind public messages.

Through the Puzzle Plan, this reading functions as protective literacy because students learn to recognize and interrupt narrow narratives.

### 7. Common Sense Digital Citizenship Reading Transcript

This source frames digital citizenship as literacy. Students are not only using devices; they are reading digital spaces, interpreting messages, making choices, and participating in communities.

Through EQUITAS, the source asks teachers not to assume all students have the same digital access, guidance, confidence, or emotional safety online.

### 8. Common Sense Digital Citizenship Themed Summary

This source deepens digital citizenship by focusing on identity, stereotype pressure, confirmation bias, community responsibility, and upstander literacy. Students learn that digital participation shapes how people and groups are seen.

Through the course theme, this source connects digital citizenship to dignity, response, and classroom community.

### 9. Nichole Pinkard

This media source frames digital literacy as an ecosystem of learning opportunities. Students need access not only to devices, but also to mentors, communities, authentic audiences, creative tools, and feedback.

Through the Puzzle Plan, Pinkard's work connects access to agency, joy, and production.

## 4. Cross-Source Thematic Synthesis

### Digital literacy is social practice

Across the sources, digital literacy is not simply skill with technology. It is a way of communicating, interpreting, creating, belonging, questioning, collaborating, and participating.

### Digital literacy expands what counts as literacy

The module expands literacy beyond reading and writing to include digital composing, visual design, social media communication, news analysis, online identity, gaming, multimodal production, peer teaching, and community participation.

### Digital literacy can reproduce or interrupt inequity

Digital tools do not automatically create equity. They can reproduce exclusion when they assume stable access, English dominance, fast processing, emotional regulation, or prior digital confidence. They can interrupt inequity when they are designed for access, multiple modes, care, and critical awareness.

### Digital citizenship is critical literacy

Digital citizenship is not only online safety. It includes privacy, audience, media balance, identity, representation, confirmation bias, community responsibility, and upstander communication.

### Media literacy is protective literacy

Students need tools to analyze misinformation, stereotypes, missing voices, and public narratives. This is especially important for students whose identities are often misread, simplified, or treated through deficit assumptions.

## 5. Puzzle Plan Connection

Module 3 supports the Puzzle Plan by showing that digital literacy can become a powerful equity system when it is designed intentionally.

- **Access:** Students need multiple pathways into digital tools, platforms, texts, and participation.
- **Identity:** Students use digital spaces to represent culture, language, interest, body, community, and belonging.
- **Healing:** Digital literacy can interrupt shame, invisibility, stereotype pressure, and forced masking when taught with care.
- **Agency:** Students create, respond, publish, remix, critique, advocate, and support classmates.
- **Criticality:** Students analyze misinformation, stereotypes, platform influence, bias, missing voices, and narrow narratives.
- **Joy:** Digital tools can support creativity, discovery, collaboration, peer teaching, STEM identity, and shared success.
- **Transformation:** Students move from passive consumers to active, critical, caring, and ethical participants.

## 6. EQUITAS Connection

EQUITAS asks teachers to examine the hidden default learner. In digital literacy, the default learner is often assumed to have stable internet, device access, English-dominant confidence, emotional regulation, family support, prior digital experience, and the ability to navigate online spaces without explicit teaching.

A Module 3 EQUITAS check asks:

1. Who has access to digital tools, feedback, mentorship, and support?
2. Who is being asked to participate in ways that may not match their language, culture, body, brain, or lived experience?
3. Who may be labeled careless, distracted, or irresponsible before the classroom asks what support they need?
4. How can students show digital understanding through multiple modes?
5. How can media literacy help students recognize and challenge narrow narratives?
6. How can digital citizenship be taught without shame?
7. How can students practice upstander language before difficult digital moments happen?
8. How can technology support belonging, not just task completion?

## 7. Connection to Course Goals

The larger course goal is to understand literacy as culturally and historically responsive, not as a narrow print-only skill. Module 3 supports this goal by showing that digital spaces are also literacy spaces.

Students need to learn how to:

- read digital environments;
- interpret media messages;
- question bias and misinformation;
- understand privacy, audience, and visibility;
- communicate responsibly;
- participate in community;
- create and revise multimodal artifacts;
- respond as upstanders;
- create counternarratives;
- use technology for meaning-making.

This supports CHRE and CHRL because it treats students' lived experiences, languages, identities, and digital practices as part of learning.

## 8. Teaching Practice Implications

For future teaching, Module 3 suggests that educators should:

1. Teach digital citizenship as literacy, not only behavior management.
2. Use real-life scenarios from students' digital worlds.
3. Provide multiple ways to demonstrate understanding.
4. Connect digital safety to care, not fear or shame.
5. Teach students to analyze representation and missing voices.
6. Teach confirmation bias, filter bubbles, and repeated digital messages as literacy topics.
7. Treat students' digital knowledge as classroom resources.
8. Build peer teaching into digital literacy work.
9. Design for access before judging performance.
10. Connect media literacy to justice, identity, and community.
11. Help students become creators, upstanders, and critical participants, not only consumers.
12. Build digital learning ecosystems that include mentorship, audience, feedback, and authentic production.

## 9. Placement Connection

This module connects strongly to the Grade 4 Minecraft placement example. When students who finished early helped classmates continue their work, digital literacy became peer teaching and community responsibility. That moment shows what digital citizenship can look like in practice: students used technology knowledge to support others.

It also connects to Kindergarten circuits and Grade 5 airplane/fan-circuit design because those lessons expanded literacy beyond writing. Students learned through tools, collaboration, movement, materials, vocabulary, and shared problem-solving. Module 3 extends that same logic into digital spaces.

## 10. Final Module Takeaway

Module 3 shows that digital and media literacy are central to culturally and historically responsive teaching. Digital literacy is access, identity, agency, care, critical media analysis, upstander literacy, multimodal production, and community participation.

Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS, Module 3 argues that students should not be judged by narrow assumptions about digital behavior or print-only literacy. They should be taught how to use digital spaces to learn, create, protect, question, connect, respond, and transform.
