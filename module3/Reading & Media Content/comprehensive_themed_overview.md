## 1. Overall Module Frame

Module 3 focuses on digital and media literacy. Through the course theme, this module is not only about learning how students use technology. It is about understanding digital literacy as access, identity work, critical participation, community responsibility, and self-protection.

The central question is:

> How can digital and media literacy help students who are often misread by school systems become more visible, more protected, more connected, more critical, and more fully themselves?

Digital literacy is not neutral. Students enter digital spaces with different levels of access, support, language background, cultural recognition, emotional safety, and prior experience. For this reason, digital citizenship and media literacy must be taught through equity, not just rules.

## 3. Main Thematic Synthesis

### Digital citizenship as literacy

The source frames digital citizenship as a literacy practice because students are constantly reading and composing meaning in digital spaces. They interact with websites, videos, images, posts, comments, search results, messages, and online communities. These are texts, and students need support learning how to interpret them.

### Digital citizenship as access

A key theme is access. Students do not all have the same digital background, technology confidence, adult guidance, internet access, or emotional safety. An equitable digital literacy classroom should not assume that every student enters with the same tools or experiences.

### Digital citizenship as identity work

Digital spaces shape identity. Students use digital environments to communicate who they are, connect with others, protect themselves, explore interests, and respond to representation. A culturally responsive classroom should help students think about audience, privacy, visibility, and self-presentation.

### Digital citizenship as critical media literacy

The source emphasizes that students should question digital messages. They should ask who created a message, what purpose it serves, who is represented, who is missing, and what assumptions are being repeated. This turns digital citizenship into critical media literacy.

### Digital citizenship as care

Digital participation is relational. Online choices affect real people. A strong digital citizenship framework helps students practice care, responsibility, boundaries, respect, and repair.

## 4. Puzzle Plan Connection

Module 3 supports the Puzzle Plan by showing that digital literacy can be a powerful equity tool when it is designed intentionally.

- **Access:** Digital literacy gives students multiple ways to participate, communicate, and show knowledge.
- **Identity:** Students can use digital tools to explore and represent language, culture, experience, and belonging.
- **Healing:** Digital citizenship can interrupt shame-based approaches to online behavior and replace them with support, reflection, and care.
- **Agency:** Students can create, respond, publish, remix, critique, and advocate.
- **Criticality:** Students can analyze misinformation, stereotypes, media bias, missing voices, and harmful assumptions.
- **Joy:** Digital tools can support creativity, discovery, collaboration, and peer teaching.
- **Transformation:** Students move from being passive consumers to active, critical, and ethical participants.

## 5. EQUITAS Connection

EQUITAS asks teachers to examine the hidden default learner. In digital literacy, the default learner is often assumed to have stable internet, device access, English-dominant confidence, emotional regulation, family support, and prior digital experience.

A Module 3 EQUITAS check asks:

1. Who has access to digital tools and support?
2. Who is being asked to participate in ways that may not match their language, culture, body, brain, or lived experience?
3. Who may be labeled careless, distracted, or irresponsible before the classroom asks what support they need?
4. How can students show digital understanding through multiple modes?
5. How can media literacy help students recognize and challenge harmful narratives?
6. How can online safety be taught without shame?
7. How can technology support belonging, not just task completion?

## 6. Connection to Course Goals

The larger course goal is to understand literacy as culturally and historically responsive, not as a narrow print-only skill. Module 3 supports this goal by showing that digital spaces are also literacy spaces.

Students need to learn how to:

- read digital environments;
- interpret media messages;
- question bias and misinformation;
- understand privacy and audience;
- communicate responsibly;
- participate in community;
- protect themselves and others;
- create counternarratives;
- use technology for meaning-making.

This supports CHRE and CHRL because it treats students' lived experiences, languages, identities, and digital practices as part of learning.

---

## 7. Teaching Practice Implications

For future teaching, Module 3 suggests that educators should:

1. Teach digital citizenship as literacy, not only behavior management.
2. Use real-life scenarios from students' digital worlds.
3. Provide multiple ways to demonstrate understanding.
4. Connect digital safety to care, not fear or shame.
5. Teach students to analyze representation and missing voices.
6. Treat students' digital knowledge as classroom resources.
7. Build peer teaching into digital literacy work.
8. Design for access before judging performance.
9. Connect media literacy to justice, identity, and community.
10. Help students become creators and critical participants, not only consumers.

This module connects strongly to the Grade 4 Minecraft placement example. When students who finished early helped classmates continue their work, digital literacy became peer teaching and community responsibility. That moment shows what digital citizenship can look like in practice: students used technology knowledge to support others.

It also connects to Kindergarten circuits and Grade 5 airplane/fan-circuit design because those lessons expanded literacy beyond writing. Students learned through tools, collaboration, movement, materials, vocabulary, and shared problem-solving. Module 3 extends that same logic into digital spaces.

## 9. Final Module Takeaway

Module 3 shows that digital and media literacy are central to culturally and historically responsive teaching. Digital citizenship is not just online safety. It is access, identity, agency, care, critical media analysis, and community participation.

Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS, Module 3 argues that students should not be judged by narrow assumptions about digital behavior. They should be taught how to use digital spaces to learn, create, protect, question, connect, and transform.
