# Brooks & Frankel (2020): Transnational Digital Literacy

## Readable Themed Overview

Brooks and Frankel help us see that students bring digital literacies into the classroom long before school names them as academic. Their reading focuses on adolescents whose digital lives cross languages, countries, families, and communities. A student might use digital tools to communicate with relatives, translate information, follow events across borders, participate in multilingual spaces, or maintain cultural connections that school does not always recognize.

The major point is that these practices are not side activities. They are literacy. They require interpretation, audience awareness, cultural knowledge, translation, code-switching, judgment, and digital navigation. When schools ignore those practices, students can be treated as lacking even when they are already doing complex meaning-making outside the classroom.

Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS lens, this reading is about turning difference into evidence of strength. Instead of asking students to leave home language, family knowledge, and transnational identity outside the classroom, teachers should use those literacies as part of learning. This matters especially in a course focused on culturally and historically responsive literacy because students' real literacy lives often extend far beyond school walls.

## Teaching Takeaway

Teachers should invite students to use multilingual, family, cultural, and digital knowledge in academic work. Students might create bilingual digital stories, compare media across communities, analyze how an issue appears in different languages, or use digital tools to connect personal experience with classroom learning.
