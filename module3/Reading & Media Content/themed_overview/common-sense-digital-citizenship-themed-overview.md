## 1. Source Purpose

This source frames digital citizenship as a literacy practice. It argues that students are not only using technology; they are reading, interpreting, composing, responding, and participating inside digital environments. The source identifies core digital citizenship concerns such as privacy, digital footprints, online communication, media balance, misinformation, respectful participation, and safe decision-making.

The most important shift is that digital citizenship is not presented as rule-following alone. It becomes a way of helping students participate online with care, responsibility, safety, critical awareness, and agency.

---

## 2. Main Ideas

### Digital citizenship is literacy

Students are constantly reading digital spaces: websites, videos, posts, comments, search results, messages, images, games, and online communities. Digital citizenship teaches students how to make meaning in those spaces and how to respond with care and judgment.

### Digital spaces are not neutral

Students enter digital spaces with different levels of access, language support, adult guidance, background knowledge, confidence, and safety. A culturally responsive approach cannot assume a default digital learner.

### Digital citizenship requires critical questioning

Students need to ask who created a message, what purpose it serves, who is represented, who is missing, and what assumptions are being repeated. This connects digital citizenship to critical media literacy.

### Digital citizenship includes agency

The goal is not only to prevent online harm. The goal is to help students become thoughtful, critical, caring, and empowered digital participants.

---

## 3. Puzzle Plan Connection

This source fits the Puzzle Plan because it treats digital citizenship as a system of access, identity, agency, criticality, and care.

- **Access:** Students need explicit support to participate safely and meaningfully in digital environments.
- **Identity:** Digital spaces shape how students present themselves and how they are represented by others.
- **Agency:** Students should learn to create, respond, question, and participate rather than only consume media.
- **Criticality:** Students need tools to analyze misinformation, representation, bias, missing voices, and repeated assumptions.
- **Joy:** Digital tools can support creativity, collaboration, peer teaching, and shared discovery when they are designed with care.
- **Transformation:** Digital citizenship can move students from passive users of technology to critical participants who understand their power and responsibility.

---

## 4. EQUITAS Connection

Through EQUITAS, this source asks teachers to examine who is being treated as the default digital learner. A lesson that assumes every student has stable access, adult support, emotional safety, fast processing, and English-dominant confidence will reproduce inequity.

An EQUITAS-aligned digital citizenship lesson should ask:

1. Who has access to the tools needed to participate?
2. Who may be misread as careless when they actually need support?
3. Whose language, culture, identity, body, or lived experience shapes digital participation?
4. What supports make safe participation possible?
5. How can students show digital understanding through multiple modes?
6. How can students learn to critique harmful media narratives without internalizing them?

## 5. Course Theme Connection

This source connects strongly to EDU498 because it expands literacy beyond print. Digital citizenship includes reading systems, interpreting online messages, understanding audience, protecting self and others, building community, and questioning power.

For students who are often misread by school systems, digital citizenship can become protective literacy. It gives students language for privacy, boundaries, representation, misinformation, digital pressure, community responsibility, and self-advocacy.

---

## 6. Teaching Implications

A teacher using this source should:

- teach digital citizenship as literacy, not only behavior management;
- use real digital scenarios students recognize;
- invite students to analyze representation and missing perspectives;
- make privacy and safety conversations supportive instead of shame-based;
- provide multiple ways for students to show understanding;
- connect digital responsibility to classroom community;
- treat students' existing digital practices as resources;
- design for access before judging participation.

---

## 7. Placement Connection

This source connects to the Grade 4 Minecraft placement example because students used digital skills to support classmates. When students who finished early helped classmates continue their work, digital literacy became community responsibility rather than individual completion.

It also connects to the broader placement pattern in which students learn through tools, collaboration, movement, peer support, and shared problem-solving. Digital citizenship should be taught the same way: through practice, relationships, and meaningful participation.

---

## 8. Final Themed Takeaway

Digital citizenship is not an extra technology lesson. It is a form of literacy that helps students read digital spaces, understand power and representation, protect themselves and others, and participate with care, criticality, and agency. Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS, digital citizenship becomes part of building classrooms where students are not punished for difference but supported into meaningful participation.
