## Source Purpose

This source reads Common Sense Education's Digital Citizenship Curriculum through the idea of literacy as critical participation in digital spaces. It focuses on how students build identity online, how digital messages shape assumptions, how information habits influence belief, and how students can respond with care and responsibility.

The source is useful for EDU498 because it treats digital citizenship as more than internet safety. It frames digital citizenship as identity work, power awareness, media analysis, and community responsibility.

---

## Main Ideas

### Identity and digital representation

The source emphasizes that students are read by others in digital spaces. Profile choices, audience reactions, repeated labels, platform signals, and peer language all shape how identity is interpreted. This makes digital citizenship part of literacy because students must learn to read digital environments and understand how meaning is produced.

### Community responsibility

The source treats online interaction as a community issue. Students need language for noticing when communication becomes unfair, exclusionary, or harmful, and they need practice responding with clarity and care.

### Information habits and confirmation bias

The source connects filter bubbles, click-based media, repeated claims, and false information to the way assumptions become reinforced. When students repeatedly encounter the same kind of message, that repetition can make narrow or inaccurate ideas feel normal.

### Upstander literacy

The source frames response as a literacy practice. Students need words, judgment, timing, and responsibility to respond when digital spaces become unsafe or unfair. Upstander literacy is not only about being kind; it is about knowing how to participate in a community with courage and care.

## Puzzle Plan Connection

This source fits the Puzzle Plan because it shows digital citizenship as access, identity, agency, criticality, and care.

- **Access:** Students need explicit support to understand digital participation and platform influence.
- **Identity:** Students need tools to think about how belonging and representation are shaped online.
- **Healing:** Students need classroom language that interrupts shame, silence, and isolation.
- **Agency:** Students become participants who can question, respond, and support others.
- **Criticality:** Students learn to question repeated messages, narrow assumptions, and misleading information.
- **Joy:** Safer digital communities make creativity, collaboration, and belonging more possible.
- **Transformation:** Students move from passive digital use to thoughtful participation and community care.

---

## EQUITAS Connection

Through EQUITAS, this source asks teachers to examine the hidden default digital learner. Schools often assume students already know how to evaluate online information, manage visibility, and respond to difficult digital moments. That assumption is not equitable.

An EQUITAS-aligned reading asks:

1. Who is expected to understand digital norms without being taught?
2. Who is most likely to be misread in digital spaces?
3. What platform features shape visibility, belonging, and exclusion?
4. What language do students need to respond safely and clearly?
5. How can digital citizenship support belonging instead of simple compliance?

---

## Course Theme Connection

This source connects directly to the EDU498 theme because it expands literacy into digital identity, media analysis, community response, and stereotype interruption. It supports culturally and historically responsive literacy by asking students to examine who is centered, who is reduced, whose evidence counts, and what kinds of response create care.

For students who are often misread by school systems, this matters because digital spaces can repeat the same narrow narratives that exist offline. Digital citizenship becomes a way to protect dignity, recognize unfair patterns, and build more responsible communities.

---

## Teaching Implications

A teacher using this source should:

- teach students to analyze how identity is represented online;
- connect repeated digital messages to assumption-building;
- teach confirmation bias and filter bubbles as literacy topics;
- help students practice response language before difficult moments happen;
- create classroom routines for digital communication and repair;
- connect digital citizenship to CHRE/CHRL, not only online safety.

---

## Placement Connection

This source connects to the Grade 4 Minecraft placement example because digital participation became community responsibility. Students who understood the task helped classmates continue their work. That moment showed digital literacy as peer support, access, and shared success.

It also connects to the broader placement theme that literacy can be collaborative, technical, relational, and action-based. Students learn digital citizenship best when they practice responsibility, not only hear rules about responsibility.

---

## Final Themed Takeaway

Digital citizenship is literacy for identity, media awareness, community care, and critical participation. Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS, this source shows that students need tools to question digital systems, recognize unfair patterns, and participate in ways that protect dignity and belonging.
