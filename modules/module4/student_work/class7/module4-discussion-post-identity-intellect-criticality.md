## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

### Quote 1 — Moje (2015): Intellect Includes Emotion, Curiosity, Voice, and Meaning

> *"students are developing scholars with emotion and curiosity and human beings who need voice, agency, and meaning even as they learn how to enter the discourse community and culture of a discipline."*

**Source:** Moje, E. B. (2015). *Doing and teaching disciplinary literacy with adolescent learners: A social and cultural enterprise.* Harvard Educational Review, 85(2), 254–278.

This challenges how often schools define intelligence through speed, memory, or a polished final answer. For me, intellect is also pattern recognition, questioning, connection-making, creativity, persistence, and the ability to revise thinking. That matters for all students, even more to neurodivergent and CLD students — they may think deeply but need different pacing, tools, or ways to show what they know. This spoke to me because I have seen how a classroom can misread a student the same way a system can misclassify data: if the evidence is too narrow, the conclusion becomes unfair.

---

### Quote 2 — Malloy, Tracy, Scales, Menickelli & Scales (2020): Argument Is Not About Being Right

> *"a good argument is about more than fighting or being right. A well-structured argument, like the kind you have in a debate, can help you solve problems."*

**Source:** Malloy, J. A., Tracy, K. N., Scales, R. Q., Menickelli, K., & Scales, W. D. (2020). It’s not about being right: Developing argument through debate. *Journal of Literacy Research, 52*(1), 79–100.

This quote reframes argument as a literacy practice instead of a competition. Debate and discussion should not only reward the loudest, fastest, or most confident student. When structured well, argument can help students practice listening, evidence, reasoning, disagreement, revision, and perspective-taking. This made me think about how arguments can either open or close access. If a discussion only values quick verbal responses, some students may be treated as less intellectual even when they are doing serious thinking internally. But if students have preparation time, roles, visual supports, writing options, or small-group rehearsal, argument becomes a way to grow intellect.

---

### Quote 3 — Nogueron-Liu, Shimek & Bahlmann Bollinger (2018): “Tell Me What It Was About”

> *"interactions show how children talk about books when mothers say ‘Dime de que se trató’ [Tell me what this was about]."*

**Source:** Nogueron-Liu, S., Shimek, C. H., & Bahlmann Bollinger, C. (2018). ‘Dime de que se trató’ / Tell me what it was about: Exploring emergent bilinguals’ linguistic resources in reading assessments with parent participation. *Early Childhood Literacy, 20*(2), 411–433.

This sounds simple, but it points to a much deeper assessment issue. When students are allowed to use their full linguistic resources, families, oral explanation, or other ways of meaning-making, teachers may see comprehension that a narrow assessment would hide. This connects strongly to multilingual and culturally responsive teaching. A student may understand a story, concept, system, or problem deeply, but if the only accepted evidence is one form of academic English, then the assessment may measure language gatekeeping more than intellect. For me, this is where literacy and equity meet: assessment should reveal student knowledge, not bury it.

## 2 Questions I Have

### Question 1: How do we document disciplinary literacy when the evidence looks like a conversation, not a paragraph?

In my Grade 4 Minecraft placement, a student could not crack a timed dual-plate challenge. I sat with him, counted blocks aloud together — *one, two, three, four* — revised the agent’s path three times, debugged why the turn was wrong, and eventually watched him run the code and make it in under twenty seconds. That is disciplinary thinking: sequencing logic, spatial reasoning, revision under constraint, and persistent problem-solving. But none of it produced a written artifact. If the only valid evidence of literacy in that lesson had been a paragraph summary, that student disappears.

Moje argues that disciplinary literacy involves entering a discourse community through its ways of knowing, doing, and communicating. I believe it also involves the emotional and relational work that surrounds that entry — the debugging, the counting together, the peer teaching that followed. The question I keep returning to is: how do we build assessment systems that capture that kind of disciplinary thinking without forcing it into a single format? If a student teaches a classmate how to navigate the agent, argues a better route, or fixes their own logic after seeing where it breaks, that is argument and disciplinary literacy. It just does not look like a five-paragraph essay.

### Question 2: How do we teach students to enter disciplinary communities without erasing the knowledge they already carry?

Moje asks students to learn to navigate the language, tools, and norms of a discipline. That is necessary. But Baker-Bell reminds us that disciplinary language communities have historically demanded linguistic assimilation as the price of entry. Nogueron-Liu shows us that a mother asking *“Dime de que se trató”* is not outside the literacy — she is part of it. Her question surfaces comprehension that the English-only assessment missed entirely.

In my placement, some of the richest disciplinary reasoning happened in students’ first languages, in gestures, in spatial problem-solving with no text at all. The challenge is not getting students to sound like disciplinary insiders. The challenge is designing entry points wide enough that the knowledge students already carry is visible, named, and treated as part of the discipline rather than noise to be cleaned up before the real work starts. I do not have a clean answer to this yet. But I think the starting point is refusing to treat comprehension and communication as the same thing.

## 1 Connection to My Teaching Practice / Future Classroom

These readings pushed me to think about how disciplinary literacy is often taught as assimilation — students learn to sound like historians, scientists, or mathematicians — but the strongest literacy moments in my placement happened when students did not assimilate: they built, counted, coded, argued, debugged, and taught one another in their own voice.

In the Grade 4 Minecraft agent challenge at Pine Brook Elementary, a student and I spent over fifteen minutes counting blocks, revising turn directions, and testing logic under a twenty-second timer. He was practicing the same intellectual moves a programmer or engineer uses: decompose the problem, test a hypothesis, read the error, revise, rerun. He was not writing about those moves. He was doing them. When a student who finished early was redirected to help a peer who was stuck, the classroom shifted from a space of individual performance to a space of shared disciplinary practice. That is closer to how disciplines actually work — knowledge is produced collaboratively, through argument and revision, not through isolated written demonstration.

For my future classroom, I want to design tasks where disciplinary thinking is the goal but the pathway in is never one format, one language, or one pace. I want to build in multiple entry points for showing understanding: spatial, oral, computational, collaborative, and written. I also want to build in critical questions from the start — not just *can you do this* but *who built these tools, whose problems do they solve, and what does this system assume about the person using it?* That is where disciplinary literacy and critical literacy meet. And based on what I have seen in my placement, students are already asking those questions. They just need a classroom that is designed to hear them.
