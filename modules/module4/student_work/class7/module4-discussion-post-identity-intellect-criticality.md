## Three (3) Quotes That Stood Out to Me the Most

### Quote 1 — Moje, E. B. (2015): Intellect Includes Emotion, Curiosity, Voice, and Meaning

> *"students are developing scholars with emotion and curiosity and human beings who need voice, agency, and meaning …"*

**Source:** Moje, E. B. (2015). *Doing and teaching disciplinary literacy with adolescent learners: A social and cultural enterprise.* Harvard Educational Review, 85(2), 254–278.

This challenges how often schools define intelligence through speed, memory, or a polished final answer. For me, intellect is also pattern recognition, questioning, connection-making, creativity, persistence, and the ability to revise thinking. That matters for all students, even more to neurodivergent and CLD students — they may think deeply but need different pacing, tools, or ways to show what they know. This spoke to me because I have seen how a classroom can misread a student the same way a system can misclassify data: if the evidence is too narrow, the conclusion becomes unfair.

---

### Quote 2 — Malloy, Tracy, Scales, Menickelli & Scales, W. D. (2020): An Argument Is Not About Being Right

> *"a good argument is about more than fighting or being right …"*

**Source:** Malloy, J. A., Tracy, K. N., Scales, R. Q., Menickelli, K., & Scales, W. D. (2020). It's not about being right: Developing argument through debate. *Journal of Literacy Research, 52*(1), 79–100.

This quote reframes argument as a literacy practice instead of a competition. Debate and discussion should not only reward the loudest, fastest, or most confident student. When structured well, argument can help students practice listening, evidence, reasoning, disagreement, revision, and perspective-taking. This made me think about how arguments can either open or close access. If a discussion only values quick verbal responses, some students may be treated as less intellectual even when they are doing serious thinking internally. But if students have preparation time, roles, visual supports, writing options, or small-group rehearsal, argument becomes a way to grow intellect.

---

### Quote 3 — Nogueron-Liu, Shimek & Bahlmann Bollinger (2018): "Tell Me What It Was About"

> *"interactions show how children talk about books when mothers say 'Dime de que se trató'."*

**Source:** Nogueron-Liu, S., Shimek, C. H., & Bahlmann Bollinger, C. (2018). 'Dime de que se trató' / Tell me what it was about: Exploring emergent bilinguals' linguistic resources in reading assessments with parent participation. *Early Childhood Literacy, 20*(2), 411–433.

This sounds simple, but it points to a much deeper assessment issue. When students are allowed to use their full linguistic resources, families, oral explanation, or other ways of meaning-making, teachers may see comprehension that a narrow assessment would hide. This connects strongly to multilingual and culturally responsive teaching. A student may understand a story, concept, system, or problem deeply, but if the only accepted evidence is one form of academic English, then the assessment may measure language gatekeeping more than intellect. For me, this is where literacy and equity meet: assessment should reveal student knowledge, not bury it.

## Two (2) Questions I Have

### Q1: How do we document disciplinary literacy when the evidence looks like a conversation, not a paragraph?

In my Grade 4 Minecraft placement at Pine Brook Elementary, a student could not crack a timed dual-plate challenge. We sat together, counted blocks aloud; one, two, three, four, revised the agent's path multiple times, debugged why the turn direction was wrong, and eventually watched him run the code and make it under twenty seconds. That is disciplinary thinking: spatial reasoning, logical sequencing, revision under constraint, and persistent problem-solving. But none of it produced a written artifact. If the only valid evidence of learning in that lesson had been a paragraph summary, that student disappears from the record entirely.

Moje argues that disciplinary literacy involves entering a discourse community through its ways of knowing, doing, and communicating. But, I believe it also includes the emotional and relational work that surrounds that entry; the debugging, the counting together, the moment a student who finished early was redirected to sit beside a peer and become the explainer. That peer teaching is an argument. That revision is disciplinary reasoning. The question I keep returning to is: how do we build assessment systems that are wide enough to capture that kind of thinking, without collapsing it into a single standardized format? And who gets erased when we do not?

---

### Q2: How do we teach students to enter disciplinary communities without requiring them to abandon the knowledge they already carry?

Moje asks students to learn the language, tools, and norms of a discipline. That is necessary and real. But, Baker-Bell reminds us that disciplinary language communities have historically demanded linguistic assimilation as the price of entry, that students must leave parts of themselves at the door to be counted as knowers. Nogueron-Liu complicates this further: a mother asking "Dime de que se trató" is not outside the literacy event. She is part of it. Her question surfaces comprehension that the English-only assessment missed entirely.

In my placement, some of the richest disciplinary reasoning happened through spatial problem-solving, through gesture, through code, and through first-language explanation with no written text at all. In my airplane and fan-circuit lessons, students worked through roles and argued over design decisions without writing a single sentence of academic English, and the plane either turned on or it did not. The challenge is not getting students to perform like disciplinary insiders. The challenge is designing entry points wide enough that what students already know is visible, named, and treated as part of the discipline rather than noise that needs to be cleaned up before the real work begins. I do not have a clean answer yet. But, I think it starts with refusing to treat comprehension and formal communication as the same thing.

## One (1) Connection to My Teaching Practice / Future Classroom

These readings pushed me to think about how disciplinary literacy is often taught as assimilation; students learn to sound like historians, scientists, or mathematicians, but the strongest literacy moments in my placement happened when students did not assimilate. They built, counted, coded, argued, debugged, and taught one another in their own voice and at their own pace.

In one of my Minecraft lessons, a student and I spent over fifteen minutes counting blocks, revising turn directions, testing logic under a twenty-second timer, and rerunning code that kept failing for different reasons each time. He was doing the same intellectual moves a programmer or engineer uses: decompose the problem, test a hypothesis, read the error, revise, rerun. None of it produced a written product. When a student who had finished early was redirected to help a peer who was still stuck, the classroom shifted from a space of individual performance to a space of shared disciplinary practice, which is closer to how disciplines actually work. Knowledge is produced collaboratively, through argument and revision, not through isolated written demonstration.

Similarly, in my airplane-building lesson, students worked in roles; wings, body, fan connection, switch, and the test of understanding was whether the motor turned on. That is a different kind of disciplinary argument: not a debate about the right answer, but a shared claim made through design, tested by whether the thing works. Both lessons showed me that disciplinary literacy and joyful literacy are not separate goals. When students had agency, roles, a problem worth solving, and a reason to revise, the disciplinary thinking followed.

In the near future, I want to design tasks where disciplinary thinking is the goal but the pathway is never one format, one language, or one pace. I want to build in multiple modes for showing understanding: spatial, oral, computational, collaborative, and written, and I want those modes to be treated as equally valid, not as scaffolds toward eventual academic writing. I also want to build in critical questions from the start: not just can you do this but who built these tools, whose problems do they solve, and what does this system assume about the person using it? That is where disciplinary literacy and critical literacy converge. And based on what I have seen in my placement, students are already asking those questions. They just need a classroom designed to hear them.
