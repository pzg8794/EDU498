**The Interplay of Love, Adversity, and Disciplinary Skills**  
**Name:** Piter

## Question 1

**Think about a time when you experienced or witnessed love emerging through adversity. How did that experience impact your perspective on love?**

The first and most defining experience I carry is losing my mother, Miladis, when I was five years old. She was gone before I could fully understand what was happening, yet she had already planted something in me: curiosity, drive, and a belief that knowledge was one of the greatest gifts you could give a child. I named my company after her. Every system I build, every algorithm I design to be more fair, and every tool I create to help families are acts of love I send forward in her name. That experience reshaped how I understand love entirely. Love is not the absence of loss or struggle; it is struggle transformed into something that outlives the person who started it. Adversity did not diminish the love she left me with. It compressed it into something so concentrated that it became the engine of everything I do.

## Question 2

**Identify a core skill in your discipline. Reflect on how this skill can connect to the concept of love, especially in moments of challenge.**

My discipline sits at the intersection of machine learning, data and computer science, and education. A core skill in this work is algorithmic fairness: the practice of interrogating a system to ask, Who does this serve, and who does it leave out? In computer and data science, this means examining distributions, testing for disparate impact, and redesigning models when they reproduce harm. In moments of adversity, when a student is told by a diagnostic system that they are below grade level, or when a family is priced out of a service by an opaque algorithm, algorithmic fairness becomes a technical expression of love. It says: I refuse to build something that does not see you. The connection to love is not only metaphorical; it is structural. Caring enough to fix what is broken in a system is one of the hardest kinds of love because it requires skill, persistence, and the willingness to name injustice.

## Prompt 1

**How does adversity influence the expression of love within your disciplinary teaching?**

In computer science education, the adversity I carry as a bilingual immigrant who learned to code almost like a third language makes my teaching an act of deliberate love. When I encounter a student who is told they “do not belong” in a computational space because of how they talk, where they are from, or how their brain works, my instinct is shaped by every barrier I had to dismantle for myself. Adversity has taught me that the dominant framing of “rigor” in STEM is often a gatekeeping myth. Sometimes what we call difficulty is really exclusion wearing a merit badge. My disciplinary love, shaped by that adversity, expresses itself through designing access into instruction from the beginning: multiple entry points, multiple languages of representation, and a refusal to treat struggle as a sign of incapacity. As the work on disciplinary literacy reminds us, literacy is always social and cultural, and so is learning to think computationally. Adversity taught me to love the student standing at the door before they have proven they “deserve” to be inside.

## Prompt 2

**How can adversity foster growth in students’ understanding of both disciplinary skills and life skills?**

Adversity, when held within a community of care, is one of the most powerful learning architectures we have. In computer science, debugging is a perfect example. A model that fails is not a dead end; it is an invitation to understand the world more honestly. When I teach students to read error messages, question why their training data produced a skewed output, or notice who is absent from a dataset, I am teaching a life skill disguised as a technical one: the capacity to sit with what is broken and not give up on it. Matt de la Peña’s discussion of love resonates here because he explains that love becomes more truthful when it acknowledges adversity instead of pretending struggle does not exist. When a student in my classroom runs an analysis that produces an unfair result and then has to reckon with why, they are not just learning statistics. They are learning to become the kind of person who does not look away from hard things. That is what adversity, when properly held in the classroom, can teach: disciplinary skill and moral courage are connected muscles.
