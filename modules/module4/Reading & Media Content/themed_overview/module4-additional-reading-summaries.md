## Muhammad (2020), Cultivating Genius Chapter 4: Skills

Chapter 4 supports the skills pursuit. The key teaching idea is that skill development matters, but skills should never be separated from identity, intellect, criticality, and joy. Skill instruction becomes inequitable when it is reduced to isolated drills or when students are judged as deficient for not already knowing hidden academic expectations.

Through the Puzzle Plan, skills are part of access. Students need explicit instruction in language, genres, tools, vocabulary, disciplinary habits, and classroom expectations. Through EQUITAS, the teacher asks whether the skill is being taught clearly or whether students are being punished for not already having it.

Placement connection: in the circuit lesson, students learned vocabulary and concepts through materials, touch, conversation, and testing. In Minecraft, students learned sequencing and computational logic through problem-solving and peer support.

## Damico, Baildon, and Panos (2020): Climate Justice Literacy

This reading connects literacy to ecological, social, and political responsibility. Climate justice literacy asks students to examine the stories people live by: the narratives, metaphors, assumptions, and language patterns that shape how humans understand the environment and responsibility.

The important Module 4 connection is that literacy is not only personal expression. It is also the ability to analyze the stories that shape society. Climate literacy includes reading scientific information, media language, social narratives, and unequal impacts. This connects to criticality because students need to ask who is harmed, who benefits, who is blamed, and whose futures are being protected.

Puzzle Plan connection: this source supports criticality, agency, and transformation. EQUITAS connection: climate justice literacy asks whether students have access to language for naming systemic harm, not only individual environmental behavior.

Teaching implication: students can analyze headlines, images, community maps, weather events, political speeches, and everyday phrases about nature and progress.

## Spycher, Girard, and Moua (2020): Culturally Sustaining Disciplinary Language and Literacy for Hmong-American Children

This reading connects disciplinary literacy to culturally sustaining pedagogy. The core idea is that students' home languages, cultural practices, and community knowledge should not be treated as obstacles to disciplinary learning. Instead, they can become resources for learning academic language and disciplinary concepts.

For Module 4, this source helps link skills to identity. Students need disciplinary language, but they should not have to abandon cultural or linguistic identity to gain it. Teaching should create bridges between community knowledge and school knowledge.

Puzzle Plan connection: access, identity, and transformation. EQUITAS connection: the teacher asks whether the lesson assumes one linguistic or cultural pathway into science, social studies, reading, or writing.

Placement connection: in Kindergarten circuits, students used physical materials and everyday talk as bridges into science vocabulary. That same principle applies across cultural and linguistic contexts.

## Muhammad (2020), Cultivating Genius Chapter 5: Intellect

Chapter 5 supports the intellect pursuit. Intellect is not only recall or correctness. It includes deep thinking, questioning, knowledge-building, interpretation, creativity, curiosity, and the ability to make connections.

For Piter's EDU498 lens, this is important because many school systems reduce intellect to fast, visible performance. Neurodivergent, chronically ill, multilingual, or trauma-impacted students may think deeply but need different pacing, tools, or modes to show it.

Puzzle Plan connection: intellect connects to agency and transformation when students are treated as thinkers rather than task-completers. EQUITAS connection: ask whether the assessment truly captures intellectual work or only captures speed, compliance, memory, or polished academic language.

Teaching implication: ask students to build, explain, question, revise, compare, debate, map, draw, code, and reflect. These can all be intellectual literacy practices.

## Malloy et al. (2020): Developing Argument Through Debate

This article frames argument as more than being right. Debate can help students learn to use evidence, listen, respond, revise, and understand multiple perspectives. The important point is that argument should be taught as a literacy practice, not treated as a personality trait or a contest of domination.

Puzzle Plan connection: agency and criticality. Students learn to make claims, support ideas, respond to others, and participate in public reasoning. EQUITAS connection: debate structures must be accessible. Students should not be penalized simply because they need more processing time, have anxiety, use different language practices, or communicate better through writing, visuals, or small-group talk.

Teaching implication: use structured roles, preparation time, sentence frames, collaborative debate teams, visual evidence boards, and reflection after debate.

## Metz (2017): Decentering Standard English

Metz's article helps teachers address concerns about decentering standard English. The key issue is that teachers often worry students will be harmed if they are not taught dominant language expectations. The more justice-oriented answer is not to hide standardized English from students, but to teach it critically and alongside respect for other language varieties.

This source pairs well with Baker-Bell. It helps make the practical case for moving away from correction-only pedagogy and toward critical language awareness.

Puzzle Plan connection: identity, access, healing, and criticality. EQUITAS connection: students should have access to dominant language tools without being told their home language is wrong.

Teaching implication: teach code-shifting, code-meshing, audience awareness, and language analysis without shaming student language.

## Nogueron-Liu, Shimek, and Bahlmann Bollinger (2020): Emergent Bilinguals and Parent Participation in Reading Assessments

This reading focuses on emergent bilinguals' linguistic resources in reading assessments with parent participation. The key Module 4 point is that assessment can either hide or reveal student knowledge depending on whether it allows students and families to use their full linguistic resources.

Through EQUITAS, this reading is especially important. If an assessment only accepts one language pathway, it may misrepresent what students understand. Parent participation can help reveal meaning-making that school-only assessment might miss.

Puzzle Plan connection: access, identity, agency, and healing. Students and families become knowledge partners rather than problems to be remediated.

Teaching implication: include home language resources, family interviews, multilingual retellings, translanguaging opportunities, and assessment notes that separate language barriers from comprehension.

## Muhammad (2020), Cultivating Genius Chapter 6: Criticality

Chapter 6 supports the criticality pursuit. Criticality asks students to read power, injustice, systems, representation, and dominant narratives. It is not simply criticizing everything. It is learning to ask deeper questions: Who benefits? Who is harmed? Whose voices are missing? What assumptions are repeated? What alternatives are possible?

For Piter's work, this connects strongly to diagnostic justice. A classroom should not only help students complete literacy tasks; it should help them question systems that misread, erase, or exclude people.

Puzzle Plan connection: criticality and transformation. EQUITAS connection: students need access to language for naming systems, not only individual effort.

Teaching implication: use texts, media, images, data, classroom examples, community issues, and student questions to analyze power in age-appropriate ways.

## Luke (2012): Critical Literacy Foundational Notes

Luke's reading gives a foundation for critical literacy. The key idea is that literacy includes examining how texts work, whose interests they serve, and how language represents the world. Critical literacy asks readers to move beyond comprehension into analysis of power and ideology.

Puzzle Plan connection: criticality, agency, and transformation. EQUITAS connection: critical literacy gives students tools to question the default rather than accept it.

Teaching implication: ask students to examine authorship, audience, missing voices, repeated patterns, assumptions, framing, and alternatives.

---

## Leland, Harste, and Huber (2005): Critical Literacy in a First-Grade Classroom

This reading is especially useful for Piter's elementary placement because it shows that critical literacy is not only for older students. Young children can ask questions about fairness, representation, voice, and possibility when teachers design the work in age-appropriate ways.

Puzzle Plan connection: access, criticality, and joy. EQUITAS connection: young students should not be underestimated. They can analyze power when teachers provide language, stories, visuals, and collaborative routines.

Placement connection: Kindergarten students can discuss who gets to participate, who helps, what makes a design work for everyone, and how materials can be shared fairly.

---

## Huang (2019): Critical Multimodal Reflections on the Politics of English

Huang's article connects critical literacy with multimodality and English language learning. The key theme is that students can use multiple modes to reflect on the politics of English, including images, design, language, and composition.

Puzzle Plan connection: multimodal access, identity, and criticality. EQUITAS connection: students should not have to rely only on standard academic prose to analyze language power.

Teaching implication: let students create visual, digital, oral, and written reflections about how English shapes opportunity, identity, and power.
