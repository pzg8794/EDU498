# Moje (2015): Disciplinary Literacy as a Social and Cultural Enterprise

## Source Location

- Module 4 / Class 8 assigned reading
- Moje, E. B. (2015). "Doing and teaching disciplinary literacy with adolescent learners: A social and cultural enterprise." *Harvard Educational Review, 85*(2), 254-278.

## Source Purpose

Moje's disciplinary literacy article helps Module 4 expand from identity into skill. The key idea is that disciplinary literacy is not only learning vocabulary or procedures in a subject. It is learning how people in a discipline ask questions, build evidence, argue, represent knowledge, and participate in communities of practice.

For Piter's EDU498 work, this matters because students need more than generic reading strategies. They need access to the ways scientists, historians, mathematicians, artists, writers, coders, designers, and engineers make meaning. But this access should not require students to erase their own languages, identities, or ways of thinking.

## Main Ideas

Disciplinary literacy treats literacy as situated. Reading in science is not the same as reading in history. Explaining a circuit is not the same as interpreting a poem. Debugging a Minecraft command is not the same as writing a literary analysis. Each discipline has tools, questions, genres, evidence rules, language patterns, and ways of deciding what counts as knowledge.

Moje's social and cultural framing is important because disciplinary literacy is not neutral. Disciplines are shaped by histories of inclusion and exclusion. Students need access to disciplinary language, but they also need opportunities to question whose knowledge counts inside each field. In other words, students should learn how to enter disciplinary conversations without being told that their home languages, community knowledge, cultural experiences, or embodied ways of learning are inferior.

This is a strong bridge between skill and identity. Skills are not just technical steps. Skills are ways of participating. A student who learns to read a diagram, design a test, explain a pattern, ask a scientific question, or use evidence in a debate is also learning to see themselves as someone who belongs in that knowledge world.

## Puzzle Plan Connection

Through the Puzzle Plan, this source connects skill, access, identity, agency, and transformation.

- **Skill:** Students need explicit teaching of how literacy works inside different subjects.
- **Access:** Teachers cannot assume students already know hidden disciplinary expectations.
- **Identity:** Students need to see themselves as capable participants in disciplinary communities.
- **Agency:** Disciplinary literacy gives students tools to ask better questions and make stronger claims.
- **Transformation:** Students move from completing school tasks to participating in real knowledge-making practices.

For Piter, this connects to computer science and bioinformatics. A student does not simply "read code." They trace logic, identify relationships, test assumptions, debug errors, and explain systems. Those are disciplinary literacies. In the same way, Kindergarten students working with circuits are beginning to practice scientific and design literacy before they have formal academic language for it.

## EQUITAS Connection

EQUITAS asks whether disciplinary literacy is being gatekept or taught. Many classrooms punish students for not already knowing how academic disciplines communicate. An equitable classroom makes those hidden expectations visible.

An EQUITAS-aligned disciplinary literacy lesson would:

1. Teach the vocabulary, symbols, tools, and genres of the discipline explicitly.
2. Let students use multiple modes to approach disciplinary ideas.
3. Honor students' existing knowledge as part of the learning process.
4. Separate conceptual understanding from narrow language performance when appropriate.
5. Help students move gradually toward disciplinary precision without shaming their starting points.
6. Ask who has historically been welcomed into the discipline and who has been excluded.

This matters for students who are multilingual, neurodivergent, chronically ill, disabled, or navigating trauma. If a student cannot perform the expected academic form immediately, that does not mean they lack disciplinary intelligence.

## Course Theme Connection

Moje's article connects to Muhammad's skills pursuit. Skills are necessary, but they should not be separated from identity, intellect, criticality, and joy. Students need to learn disciplinary practices because those practices give them power. But skill-building should be culturally sustaining and historically aware.

This also connects to literacy as social practice. A disciplinary skill is not just a private ability inside a student's head. It is a way of participating in a community with tools, texts, conversations, and norms.

## Teaching Implications

A teacher using Moje's disciplinary literacy framework might:

1. Name the literacy demands of each subject area.
2. Model how experts read, write, speak, and think in that discipline.
3. Use sentence frames and vocabulary support without reducing student thinking.
4. Invite students to use drawing, building, coding, discussion, gesture, and oral explanation as bridges into disciplinary language.
5. Teach students to critique disciplines, not only enter them.
6. Treat disciplinary literacy as access to power.

## Placement Connection

The Kindergarten circuit lesson is a clear example of early disciplinary literacy. Students used the words circuit, wire, battery, switch, electricity, light, and fan while physically testing how the system worked. That was not separate from literacy. It was science literacy through embodied design.

The Grade 4 Minecraft agent challenge also fits. Students had to read the digital environment, interpret instructions, debug actions, understand sequence, and explain the logic of movement. A student who helped a peer was practicing disciplinary language and computational thinking in a social context.

These examples show why disciplinary literacy must be multimodal. Students often understand systems before they can explain them in polished academic language. A strong teacher builds the bridge from action to language, not the other way around.

## Final Themed Takeaway

Moje helps us see that skills are forms of participation. Disciplinary literacy should not be used as a gatekeeping system where only students already fluent in school language succeed. It should be taught as access: a way for students to enter, question, and transform knowledge communities while bringing their identities with them.
