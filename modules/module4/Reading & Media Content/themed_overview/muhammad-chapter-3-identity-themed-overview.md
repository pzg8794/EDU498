# Muhammad (2020), Cultivating Genius Chapter 3: Identity

## Source Location

- Module 4 / Class 7 assigned reading
- Muhammad, G. (2020). *Cultivating Genius*, Chapter 3, pp. 18-37.

## Source Purpose

Chapter 3 gives Module 4 its central identity frame. In Muhammad's historically responsive literacy model, identity is not an add-on after skills are taught. Identity is one of the core pursuits of literacy learning. Students need opportunities to know themselves, name themselves, understand their histories, and see their lives as worthy of study.

For Piter's EDU498 work, this source matters because it moves literacy away from narrow school performance and toward self-definition. A classroom does not become culturally and historically responsive just because it includes diverse texts. It becomes responsive when students' identities, histories, languages, bodies, communities, interests, and questions are built into the purpose of literacy learning.

## Main Ideas

Muhammad's identity pursuit asks teachers to design literacy instruction that helps students answer questions such as: Who am I? Who are we? How have people like me made meaning, built knowledge, created beauty, resisted oppression, and imagined new futures? This approach treats identity as intellectual work, not simply personal sharing.

A major insight is that identity work must be grounded in dignity. Students should not be asked to expose trauma, diagnoses, family history, immigration stories, poverty, grief, or pain in order to prove that their identities matter. The teacher's responsibility is to design materials, relationships, language routines, and choices that make identity visible without making disclosure mandatory.

The identity pursuit also protects students from deficit narratives. When a student is only evaluated through one narrow academic mode, the classroom can misread them. A neurodivergent student may be read as careless. A chronically ill student may be read as inconsistent. A multilingual student may be read as less sophisticated. A student whose culture is not centered may be read as disconnected. Muhammad's identity pursuit interrupts this by widening what counts as meaningful literacy participation.

## Puzzle Plan Connection

Through the Puzzle Plan, this chapter sits at the center of the identity, healing, agency, and transformation pieces.

- **Identity:** Students are not blank learners. They bring culture, race, language, health, neurodivergence, gender, family, migration history, religion, interests, grief, creativity, and community memory.
- **Healing:** Identity-centered literacy can reduce shame when students are no longer asked to mask, erase, or translate themselves into a narrow school version of intelligence.
- **Agency:** Students gain more control over how they represent themselves and what kinds of knowledge they are allowed to contribute.
- **Transformation:** A student's difference can move from being treated as a problem to being understood as part of their brilliance.

For Piter's work, this connects directly to the idea that classrooms should stop diagnosing students through narrow evidence. Literacy should help reveal capacity rather than punish students for not fitting the expected form.

## EQUITAS Connection

Through EQUITAS, Muhammad's chapter asks: Who is the default learner in this lesson? Whose identity is assumed, whose identity is ignored, and whose identity becomes visible only as a problem?

An EQUITAS-aligned classroom would not treat identity as decoration during heritage months or isolated culture days. Instead, identity would shape choices about texts, examples, language practices, collaboration structures, assessment options, and teacher feedback. Students would have multiple ways to demonstrate knowledge: speaking, building, drawing, coding, storytelling, designing, debating, translating, mapping, performing, and reflecting.

This matters especially for neurodivergent, chronically ill, multilingual, immigrant, disabled, grieving, and trauma-impacted students. If the classroom only values fast written output, the design itself becomes a filter that hides student intelligence.

## Course Theme Connection

Module 4 moves from joy into the broader pursuits. Muhammad's identity chapter shows that joy cannot be separated from identity. Students experience deeper joy when they are recognized as whole people. They also experience deeper literacy when the work helps them understand themselves and their communities.

This connects to CHRE/CHRL because historically responsive literacy asks teachers to study the intellectual traditions of communities, not only their cultural surface features. Identity is connected to history, power, resistance, language, and self-definition.

## Teaching Implications

A teacher using this chapter might:

1. Begin units with identity-safe choices rather than forced personal disclosure.
2. Use texts and media that show many ways of being intelligent, literate, creative, and powerful.
3. Ask students to connect content to questions of self, community, power, and future possibility.
4. Offer multimodal demonstrations of understanding.
5. Notice students' existing literacies, including oral language, digital work, building, peer teaching, translation, movement, and design.
6. Avoid treating identity as a one-time prompt; instead, make it part of the classroom's everyday design.

## Placement Connection

This connects to the Kindergarten squishy circuits and Grade 4 Minecraft examples. In both cases, students were not only completing tasks. They were becoming visible as builders, explainers, collaborators, designers, and problem-solvers.

In the Kindergarten circuit lesson, students used bodies, hands, vocabulary, peer support, and shared excitement to participate. In the Minecraft lesson, a student who finished early became a peer teacher. Those moments show identity emerging through practice. Students were not required to write an identity statement. Their identities as capable learners became visible because the lesson created more than one pathway into literacy.

## Final Themed Takeaway

Muhammad's identity pursuit helps us see literacy as self-definition. Students should not have to erase themselves to be seen as literate. A culturally and historically responsive classroom designs literacy so students can become more visible, more agentic, and more fully themselves without sacrificing privacy, dignity, or access.
