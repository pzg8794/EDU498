# Skerrett (2012): "We Hatched in This Class"

## Source Location

- Module 4 / Class 7 assigned reading
- Skerrett, A. (2012). "We hatched in this class": Repositioning of identity in and beyond a reading classroom. *The High School Journal, 95*(3), 62-75.

## Source Purpose

Skerrett's article helps Module 4 move from identity as an abstract concept to identity as something that can shift inside a classroom. The title phrase, "We hatched in this class," is especially powerful because it suggests that students can emerge differently when the classroom gives them the right conditions.

For Piter's EDU498 work, this reading is useful because it connects identity to classroom design. Students are not finished when they enter the room. They are becoming. A reading classroom can either trap students in existing labels or create conditions where they can try new roles, build confidence, and be recognized differently.

## Main Ideas

The article centers repositioning. Repositioning means students can be seen differently by themselves, peers, teachers, and institutions. A student who has been positioned as disengaged might become a contributor. A student who has been positioned as weak might become insightful. A student who has been positioned only through race, language, gender, class, disability, or academic history might become visible through a richer set of practices.

This idea matters because identity is not only internal. It is relational. Students become themselves in relation to the spaces, texts, tasks, peers, and adults around them. A classroom that offers only narrow roles gives students fewer ways to become. A classroom that offers multiple roles gives students more pathways into identity.

Skerrett's work also reminds us that students' identities exist beyond the classroom. School identities travel. The way a student is recognized in one class can affect how they participate in other classes, with peers, and in their future learning. When a literacy classroom helps students feel capable, that shift can matter beyond the assignment.

## Puzzle Plan Connection

Through the Puzzle Plan, this reading connects identity, healing, joy, agency, and transformation.

- **Identity:** Students are becoming, not fixed.
- **Healing:** Repositioning can repair some of the harm caused by deficit labels.
- **Joy:** Students can experience joy when they are finally seen as capable, creative, or important.
- **Agency:** Students gain room to try new roles and author themselves differently.
- **Transformation:** The classroom becomes a place where students can emerge in new forms.

This is especially important for students who have been misread by school systems. A neurodivergent student may need a different pathway to show organization, creativity, or focus. A chronically ill student may need flexibility before their competence becomes visible. A multilingual student may need opportunities to use multiple language resources before their full literacy is recognized.

## EQUITAS Connection

EQUITAS asks whether classroom design gives students real access to recognition. Skerrett's reading pushes us to ask: What identities can students take up in this classroom? Are they only test-takers and rule-followers, or can they become designers, researchers, translators, critics, artists, coders, collaborators, peer teachers, and community members?

An EQUITAS-aligned classroom does not wait for students to prove they are worthy of better roles. It designs roles that let more students become visible. This is not about lowering expectations. It is about removing unnecessary barriers so students can show fuller forms of knowledge.

## Course Theme Connection

This reading fits Muhammad's identity pursuit because it shows that identity is cultivated through practice. Students do not only learn about identity; they live identity through literacy work. The reading also connects to CHRE/CHRL because culturally and historically responsive literacy must help students understand who they are and who they can become.

The idea of "hatching" is also connected to joy. Joy is not only fun. Joy can be the feeling of emergence, recognition, competence, and belonging after being underestimated.

## Teaching Implications

A teacher using Skerrett's article might:

1. Build classroom roles that let students be seen in multiple ways.
2. Let students move between roles: listener, speaker, designer, analyst, peer coach, artist, researcher, and questioner.
3. Use texts and tasks that invite students to revise how they understand themselves.
4. Give students opportunities to succeed publicly and collaboratively.
5. Avoid freezing students into old labels.
6. Notice identity shifts as important evidence of learning.

## Placement Connection

The Minecraft example is one of the clearest placement connections. A student who finished early became a peer teacher. That moment was not just classroom management. It was identity work. The student was repositioned as someone with expertise and responsibility. His classmate also benefited because help came through a peer relationship, not only teacher correction.

The Kindergarten squishy circuit lessons show a similar pattern. Students became scientists, designers, contributors, and collaborators through touch, language, movement, and shared success. The countdown before the fan turned on mattered because it created collective ownership. Students did not just watch science happen; they participated in making it happen.

These examples show how students can "hatch" through literacy events that are physical, technical, social, oral, visual, and joyful.

## Final Themed Takeaway

Skerrett helps us understand identity as growth under the right conditions. A literacy classroom should not only measure who students already appear to be. It should create conditions where students can become more visible, more confident, and more fully recognized by themselves and others.
