# Baker-Bell (2020): Anti-Black Linguistic Racism and English Language Arts

## Source Location

- Module 4 / Class 9 assigned reading
- Baker-Bell, A. (2020). "Dismantling anti-black linguistic racism in English language arts classrooms: Toward an anti-racist black language pedagogy." *Theory into Practice, 59*(1), 8-21.

## Source Purpose

Baker-Bell's article gives Module 4 a critical language and power frame. It challenges English language arts classrooms that treat standardized English as neutral, superior, or the only legitimate form of academic literacy. The reading is important because it shows that literacy instruction can reproduce racism when it corrects students' languages without naming the racial and political history behind those corrections.

For Piter's EDU498 work, this source helps connect literacy to diagnostic justice. If schools mistake language difference for deficiency, they misdiagnose students. They may label students as less intelligent, less academic, less professional, or less prepared when the real issue is that the classroom is organized around a narrow and racialized language standard.

## Main Ideas

Baker-Bell argues for anti-racist Black language pedagogy. The point is not simply to let students use Black Language occasionally or to celebrate linguistic diversity in a surface-level way. The deeper goal is to teach students about language, racism, power, history, and resistance.

This reading is especially important because it refuses the idea that language correction is harmless. When teachers constantly correct Black students' language toward standardized English without critical context, students may receive the message that their home language, family language, community language, and identity are inferior. That is not just a grammar issue. It is an identity and dignity issue.

The reading also gives teachers a stronger alternative. Students can learn standardized English and still understand that standardized English is not morally, intellectually, or linguistically superior. They can learn code-meshing, rhetorical choice, audience awareness, and critical language consciousness. They can study why some language varieties are rewarded and others are punished.

## Puzzle Plan Connection

Through the Puzzle Plan, this reading connects criticality, identity, healing, access, and transformation.

- **Criticality:** Students should question who decides which language counts as correct, intelligent, or professional.
- **Identity:** Students' languages are tied to community, history, family, and selfhood.
- **Healing:** Anti-racist language pedagogy can interrupt shame created by correction-only classrooms.
- **Access:** Students deserve access to dominant language forms without being told their own language is deficient.
- **Transformation:** The classroom can move from enforcing language hierarchy to analyzing and challenging it.

This connects to Piter's experience with English as a gatekeeping system. Academic English can shape how professors hear students, how intelligence is judged, and how much energy multilingual students spend translating themselves into legitimacy.

## EQUITAS Connection

Through EQUITAS, Baker-Bell's article asks teachers to audit language expectations. Who is being treated as the default speaker? Whose grammar is considered normal? Whose language is marked as incorrect, unprofessional, or less academic?

An EQUITAS-aligned language classroom would:

1. Teach standardized English as one powerful language variety, not as the only correct language.
2. Teach students the history and politics of language hierarchies.
3. Validate students' home and community languages.
4. Let students analyze how language bias works in school, work, media, and public life.
5. Separate linguistic difference from intellectual ability.
6. Support rhetorical choice without forcing identity erasure.

This matters for multilingual students, Black students, CLD students, immigrant students, neurodivergent students, and students whose speech patterns, processing styles, or communication needs do not match school norms.

## Course Theme Connection

This article connects strongly to Muhammad's pursuits of identity, skills, intellect, and criticality. Students need language skills, but those skills should be taught with critical understanding. They need intellect, but intellect should not be measured only through proximity to white mainstream language norms. They need identity, but identity cannot be honored while their languages are treated as problems.

The source also connects to culturally and historically responsive literacy because it asks teachers to study language historically. Language hierarchy did not appear naturally. It was shaped through colonialism, racism, schooling, and social power.

## Teaching Implications

A teacher using Baker-Bell's framework might:

1. Teach language variation explicitly.
2. Let students compare language varieties without ranking them as superior or inferior.
3. Discuss how racism shapes language judgments.
4. Use student language as a resource in reading, writing, speaking, and analysis.
5. Create assignments where students can use home language, academic language, code-meshing, translation, or rhetorical shifting intentionally.
6. Correct less and analyze more.

This does not mean abandoning academic language instruction. It means teaching academic language without making students believe their own language is broken.

## Placement Connection

In Piter's placement, this connects to how students explain their thinking orally, physically, digitally, and collaboratively. Kindergarten students explaining circuits may not use perfect academic language, but they are still participating in scientific literacy. Grade 4 students explaining Minecraft agent movement may use fragments, gestures, commands, pointing, peer talk, and trial-and-error language. Those forms should be recognized as meaningful literacy practices, not dismissed because they are not polished school English.

The teacher's job is to build bridges from student language to disciplinary language while protecting the dignity of the student's original expression.

## Final Themed Takeaway

Baker-Bell helps us see that language instruction is never neutral. A justice-oriented literacy classroom teaches students how language power works while refusing to shame the languages students bring. Students should gain access to dominant language tools without losing the right to speak, write, think, and belong as themselves.
