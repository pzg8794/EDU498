# Moje, Luke, Davies, and Street (2009): Literacy and Identity

## Source Location

- Module 4 / Class 7 assigned reading
- Moje, E. B., Luke, A., Davies, B., & Street, B. (2009). "Literacy and identity: Examining the metaphors in history and contemporary research." *Reading Research Quarterly, 44*(4), 415-437.

## Source Purpose

This article gives Module 4 a research-based language for understanding the relationship between literacy and identity. The authors examine how scholars have used different metaphors to explain identity in literacy research. The important point for our course is that identity is not a static trait students carry into school unchanged. Identity is shaped through social practices, texts, language, relationships, institutions, histories, and power.

For Piter's EDU498 work, this article helps explain why literacy tasks can never be neutral. Every literacy task gives students a role. A student may be positioned as capable, deficient, creative, disruptive, expert, helper, outsider, translator, designer, or problem-solver. Teachers need to notice those positionings because they affect how students see themselves and how others see them.

## Main Ideas

The article treats literacy and identity as intertwined. Students do not simply read and write as isolated individuals. They participate in social worlds. They take up roles, respond to expectations, use available languages and tools, and negotiate how they are recognized.

This matters because school often gives students identity stories before students can tell their own. A student may become "the struggling reader," "the quiet student," "the behavior problem," "the ESL student," "the gifted student," or "the student who does not try." Those labels can become powerful narratives, even when they are incomplete or wrong.

The article's metaphor-based approach is useful because different metaphors reveal different dimensions of identity:

- identity as something constructed through practice;
- identity as something performed in social interaction;
- identity as something narrated over time;
- identity as something positioned by institutions and others;
- identity as something linked to power, discourse, and recognition.

The practical message is that teachers should examine how classroom literacy practices create identities. A reading group, writing prompt, technology task, discussion protocol, or assessment can either narrow students' identities or expand them.

## Puzzle Plan Connection

Through the Puzzle Plan, this reading belongs especially to identity, agency, and healing.

- **Identity:** Students are constantly making and remaking who they are in relation to texts, tools, peers, teachers, and institutional expectations.
- **Agency:** Students need opportunities to author themselves, not only be authored by school labels.
- **Healing:** When students have been misread by school systems, literacy can become a way to revise deficit stories.
- **Criticality:** Teachers need to ask whose identity categories are being normalized and whose are being pathologized.

For Piter, this connects to neurodivergence and chronic illness. A student who looks fine but is struggling physically, emotionally, or cognitively can be misread as lazy, careless, or inconsistent. The problem is not only the student label; it is the classroom's evidence system. If only one type of literacy counts, many identities become invisible.

## EQUITAS Connection

Through EQUITAS, the article asks teachers to audit how literacy tasks position students. The question is not simply, "Did the student complete the assignment?" It is also, "What identity did this assignment make possible for the student?"

An EQUITAS-aligned lesson would ask:

1. Who gets to be seen as smart in this task?
2. Who gets to be seen as creative?
3. Who gets to be seen as literate?
4. Who is only visible when they make a mistake?
5. What modes of expression are accepted?
6. Does the task let students revise how they are seen?

This is important because assessments often misrecognize students. A student may understand the concept but struggle with the format, pacing, language, sensory demand, motor demand, or social expectation of the task. EQUITAS pushes the teacher to separate actual understanding from narrow performance conditions.

## Course Theme Connection

This source connects directly to culturally and historically responsive literacy because identity is socially, culturally, historically, and institutionally produced. Students' identities are shaped by race, language, migration, disability, class, religion, gender, family structure, health, community, and schooling history.

The article also helps connect identity to literacy as a social practice. Literacy is not only decoding words. It is participating in meaning-making systems. Students read classroom expectations, peer reactions, teacher feedback, digital tools, images, gestures, grades, and institutional signals. Those are all part of how identity forms in school.

## Teaching Implications

A teacher using this article might:

1. Replace fixed labels with flexible observations.
2. Create tasks where students can take on multiple roles: reader, builder, designer, speaker, questioner, translator, artist, researcher, peer teacher, and critic.
3. Notice how classroom routines position students.
4. Allow students to revise their work and their self-understanding.
5. Avoid using one performance as proof of a student's whole identity.
6. Design literacy work that helps students build fuller narratives of themselves.

## Placement Connection

The Grade 4 Minecraft lesson is a strong example. A student who finished early did not remain only "the student who was done." The classroom structure allowed him to become a peer teacher. That changed his identity in the moment. He became helpful, expert, responsible, and connected.

The Kindergarten squishy circuit lessons also show identity formation through multimodal participation. Students became scientists and designers not because they wrote a perfect sentence, but because they touched materials, used vocabulary, counted down together, predicted outcomes, and participated in shared success.

These examples show why identity-based literacy does not always look academic at first. Building, coding, explaining with gestures, helping peers, trial-and-error debugging, and collaborative design can all be identity-forming literacy practices.

## Final Themed Takeaway

Moje, Luke, Davies, and Street help us see that literacy tasks make identities. A classroom should not only ask what students know. It should ask what forms of selfhood the work makes possible. The goal is to design literacy spaces where students can revise deficit stories, become visible through multiple literacies, and participate without being trapped by narrow school labels.
