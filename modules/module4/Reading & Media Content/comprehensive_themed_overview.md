# Module 4 Comprehensive Themed Overview

## Module 4: Toward(s) the Pursuits

Module 4 expands the course from joy into the broader architecture of Muhammad's five pursuits: identity, skills, intellect, criticality, and joy. The central message is that literacy is not a single classroom behavior. Literacy is how students come to know themselves, participate in disciplinary communities, build language and argument, question power, and imagine more just futures.

For Piter's EDU498 work, Module 4 is especially important because it gives language to what has already been emerging in the teaching placement evidence. Kindergarten squishy circuits, Grade 4 Minecraft, and Grade 5 design lessons are not just engagement activities. They are literacy events where students become visible as builders, explainers, collaborators, designers, peer teachers, problem-solvers, and emerging disciplinary thinkers.

## Big-Picture Frame

The strongest theme across Module 4 is that literacy shapes who students are allowed to become. A classroom can treat literacy as compliance: read the text, answer the question, write the sentence, use the approved language, and perform the expected skill. But the sources in this module push toward a deeper view. Literacy is identity, skill, intellect, criticality, language power, disciplinary participation, and imagination.

Through the Puzzle Plan, Module 4 connects several pieces:

- **Identity:** students need to see themselves and be seen by others in fuller ways.
- **Access:** students need multiple routes into literacy, not one narrow school-approved format.
- **Healing:** literacy should interrupt shame, deficit labels, linguistic policing, and forced masking.
- **Agency:** students need voice, choice, authorship, and roles that let them participate meaningfully.
- **Criticality:** students need tools to question power, language hierarchy, representation, and systems.
- **Joy:** students experience joy when they feel capable, connected, and recognized.
- **Transformation:** classrooms should help students move from being misread to being understood.

Through EQUITAS, the module asks whether classroom design gives students real access, dignity, and multiple ways to demonstrate knowledge. It asks who is treated as the default learner, whose language is normalized, whose identity is visible only as a problem, and which forms of literacy are ignored.

## Most Comprehensive Source Threads

### 1. Muhammad Chapter 3: Identity

Muhammad's identity pursuit frames literacy as self-definition. Students are not blank learners who simply need skills added to them. They bring histories, languages, families, cultures, communities, bodies, health conditions, neurodivergence, grief, joy, interests, and questions. Identity-centered literacy helps students name who they are and who they are becoming.

This source is crucial because it prevents identity from becoming decorative. Identity should not be reduced to a personal prompt, a poster, or a celebration day. It should shape the texts teachers choose, the questions they ask, the ways students are allowed to respond, and the forms of knowledge that count.

For Piter, this connects directly to chronic illness, neurodivergence, and the experience of being misread by school systems. When schools use narrow evidence, they often assign deficit stories before students can tell their own. Muhammad's identity pursuit helps reframe literacy as a way to reveal capacity instead of hiding it.

### 2. Moje, Luke, Davies, and Street: Literacy and Identity

Moje, Luke, Davies, and Street help explain how literacy and identity shape each other. Their reading is useful because it treats identity as dynamic, social, and connected to power. Students are constantly being positioned through classroom tasks, language expectations, peer interactions, teacher feedback, assessments, and institutional labels.

This reading helps Piter name a major teaching question: What identity does this literacy task make possible for the student? A task can position a student as deficient, silent, behind, disruptive, or not academic. But a better-designed task can position the same student as a designer, helper, translator, speaker, builder, coder, analyst, storyteller, or peer teacher.

This connects directly to the Grade 4 Minecraft example where a student who finished early became a peer teacher. That moment matters because the classroom structure allowed his identity to shift from "done" to "expert helper." It also connects to Kindergarten circuits, where students became scientists and designers through materials, language, and shared success.

### 3. Skerrett: "We Hatched in This Class"

Skerrett's article gives Module 4 one of its strongest metaphors: students can "hatch" in a classroom. This means identity can emerge under the right conditions. Students do not enter a classroom finished. They are becoming. A strong literacy classroom gives them room to try new roles, develop confidence, and be recognized differently by themselves and others.

For Piter's placement, this reading is especially useful because it validates what happened in Minecraft and squishy circuits. Students were not simply completing activities. They were becoming visible through action. They were taking up roles: peer teacher, builder, designer, scientist, collaborator, problem-solver.

The teaching implication is that identity growth is evidence of learning. If a student gains confidence, helps a peer, uses new vocabulary, takes a risk, or sees themselves as capable in a new way, that matters. Literacy assessment should notice those changes instead of only measuring final written output.

### 4. Moje (2015): Disciplinary Literacy

Moje's disciplinary literacy reading expands the skills pursuit. Skill is not just a list of isolated academic moves. It is participation in a discipline. Students learn how scientists ask questions, how historians use evidence, how coders debug systems, how writers create voice, and how designers test solutions.

This reading helps bridge EDU498 with Piter's computer science and bioinformatics background. Coding, debugging, modeling, diagramming, testing, and explaining systems are literacy practices. They require students to read relationships, interpret symbols, use tools, revise thinking, and communicate logic.

In placement, Kindergarten circuits and Grade 4 Minecraft show disciplinary literacy in action. Students were doing science, design, and computational literacy before they could fully name those practices in polished academic language. The teacher's job is to build from action into language, not to demand polished language before students are allowed to participate.

### 5. Baker-Bell: Anti-Black Linguistic Racism

Baker-Bell's reading gives Module 4 a necessary language justice frame. It challenges classrooms that treat standard English as neutral or superior. The reading helps show that language correction is never just technical. It is tied to race, power, identity, dignity, and whose knowledge is treated as legitimate.

This source connects strongly to Piter's experience with academic English as a gatekeeping system. Students may be judged not only by what they know but by how closely their language matches dominant norms. For multilingual, Black, CLD, immigrant, neurodivergent, and disabled students, that can create constant pressure to translate themselves into acceptability.

The teaching implication is not to abandon academic language instruction. It is to teach language critically. Students deserve access to dominant language forms, but they should not be taught that their own languages are broken. They need language choice, code-meshing, rhetorical awareness, and critical language consciousness.

## Additional Source Threads

Muhammad Chapter 4 supports the skills pursuit by reminding teachers that skill development should be explicit and equitable. Students should not be punished for not already knowing hidden academic expectations.

Damico, Baildon, and Panos connect literacy to climate justice. Their work helps frame literacy as the ability to analyze the stories, metaphors, and assumptions that shape public life and ecological responsibility.

Spycher, Girard, and Moua connect disciplinary language to culturally sustaining pedagogy, especially for Hmong-American children. Their work helps show that disciplinary literacy should build from community knowledge and language resources.

Muhammad Chapter 5 supports the intellect pursuit. Intellect includes deep thinking, curiosity, knowledge-building, questioning, and creativity. It should not be reduced to speed or correctness.

Malloy and colleagues frame argument as a literacy practice. Debate is not only about winning or being right. It can teach listening, evidence, reasoning, response, and revision when structured accessibly.

Metz supports the practical case for decentering standard English while still giving students access to dominant language expectations. This pairs well with Baker-Bell.

Nogueron-Liu, Shimek, and Bahlmann Bollinger show how emergent bilingual students' linguistic resources and parent participation can reveal comprehension that narrow assessments may hide.

Luke gives the foundational critical literacy frame: students should analyze how texts represent the world, whose interests they serve, and whose voices are missing.

Leland, Harste, and Huber show that critical literacy can happen in early elementary classrooms. Young students can ask questions about fairness, representation, and voice when teachers design the work carefully.

Huang connects critical literacy to multimodal reflection and the politics of English, especially for EFL learners.

## Media Themes

The Module 4 media sources extend the reading themes into lived voice, public storytelling, and imagination.

Aaron Smoke's TEDx talk on First Nations identity and post-secondary experience supports the idea that students enter education with community histories, cultural responsibilities, and institutional pressures. It helps frame identity as continuity, not just personal preference.

The Write Time episode with Matt de la Pena and Tracey Flores connects writing to voice, relationship, story, and meaningful audience. It supports the idea that skill grows when students have real reasons to write.

Jamila Lyiscott's TEDx talk, "Why English class is silencing students of color," pairs with Baker-Bell and Metz by challenging English classrooms that silence students through narrow language norms.

The Equity Experience source with Dr. Kazembe connects critical literacy and identity for K-12 students. Its key use is helping Piter frame how even young learners can ask justice-oriented questions in age-appropriate ways.

The On Being interview with Jason Reynolds, "Fortifying Imagination," supports the idea that imagination is literacy, survival, joy, and critical possibility. Critical literacy should not only name harm. It should help students imagine otherwise.

## Placement Connection

Module 4 gives strong language for Piter's teaching placement evidence.

In Kindergarten squishy circuits, students used touch, movement, vocabulary, shared countdowns, and material design to participate in science literacy. They became scientists and contributors before they could produce formal academic explanations.

In Grade 4 Minecraft, students practiced computational literacy through sequencing, debugging, spatial reasoning, peer explanation, and persistence. The peer teacher moment shows identity repositioning in action.

In Grade 5 airplane and fan-circuit work, students practiced design literacy through collaboration, testing, role-based participation, problem-solving, and visual/material meaning-making.

These examples show that literacy can be physical, digital, relational, visual, technical, oral, and collaborative. Module 4 helps make those practices visible as identity, skill, intellect, criticality, and joy.

## Future Teaching Moves

For future classroom design, Module 4 suggests several practical moves:

1. Design identity-safe literacy tasks that honor students without requiring private disclosure.
2. Name the roles students can take up: builder, designer, critic, storyteller, scientist, translator, peer teacher, coder, artist, and questioner.
3. Teach disciplinary literacy explicitly through tools, vocabulary, examples, modeling, and multimodal practice.
4. Treat language difference as a resource, not a deficiency.
5. Build critical questions into ordinary lessons: Who is included? Who is missing? Who benefits? Who is harmed? What could be redesigned?
6. Use assessment to reveal knowledge, not hide it behind narrow performance demands.
7. Let students show intellect through building, speaking, drawing, coding, debating, mapping, revising, and collaboration.

## Module Takeaway

Module 4 shows that literacy is a pursuit of becoming. Students are not only learning to read and write. They are learning who they are, how knowledge works, how language carries power, how to participate in disciplines, how to question systems, and how to imagine futures. Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS lens, the teacher's responsibility is to design literacy environments where students do not have to erase themselves to succeed. Identity, skill, intellect, criticality, and joy should work together so students can become visible with dignity.
