## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

### Quote 1 — Knobel & Lankshear

> "Literacy is not fixed. It changes as people, tools, technologies, communities, and purposes change."

This stood out because it helped name what was already visible in my teaching placement. In the Grade 4 Minecraft activity, students were not just "playing a game." They were reading directions, interpreting a 3D space, counting steps, coding the agent, troubleshooting timing, and explaining strategies to one another. That is literacy, even though it does not look like a worksheet. The quote pushes me to ask what kinds of meaning-making we might miss when looking only for traditional reading and writing.

---

### Quote 2 — Price-Dennis, Holmes & Smith

> "Digital tools are not automatically inclusive. They become inclusive when teachers use them intentionally to reduce barriers, increase participation, and support meaningful communication."

This quote challenged me because it reminds me that access is designed — a device or tool by itself does not make a lesson equitable. In my Kindergarten circuit/fan lesson, the activity became more inclusive because students had roles: one student held the Play-Doh/conductor, another connected the wires, another controlled the switch, and the class helped answer and correct together. The joy came from the shared countdown and the fan turning on, but the access came from how the task was structured. That makes me think about how much responsibility teachers have to design the pathway into learning.

---

### Quote 3 — Melo-Pfeifer & Gertz / Pinkard

> "Do not ask only whether students can use technology. Ask whether digital literacy helps them become more visible, more critical, more connected, more protected, and more fully themselves."

This was the strongest quote — it shifts the goal of digital literacy entirely. It is not enough for students to click the right button or finish the task. The deeper question is whether digital work helps students participate with more voice and dignity. I connected this to my Grade 5 airplane/fan-circuit challenge, where students had roles like building the wings, body, wheels, and fan connection. When students finished and said, "We did it," the learning was collaborative, visible, and joyful.

## 2 Questions

### Question 1 — Assessment of Digital and Multimodal Literacy

How can teachers assess digital and multimodal literacy without forcing it into one narrow format?

If a student shows understanding through coding, building, peer teaching, oral explanation, visual design, or helping a classmate troubleshoot, how do we document that as real literacy — without reducing it to a behavior grade or making them write a paragraph afterward just to prove it counted?

---

### Question 2 — Critical Media Literacy with Young Learners

How do we teach critical media literacy to young students in a way that is honest, not overwhelming?

The readings made me think about misinformation, representation, missing voices, and stereotypes. I wonder how we can help elementary students ask questions like "Who is missing?" or "What story is being repeated?" while still keeping the learning developmentally appropriate and emotionally safe.

---

## 1 Connection to Teaching Practice / Future Classroom

The readings helped me see that digital and media literacy should be designed as access. In my teaching placement, the strongest literacy moments happened when students used tools, bodies, language, and relationships together.

In Minecraft, students read systems, debugged problems, counted steps, used commands, and taught one another. In the circuit lessons, students learned vocabulary — battery, wire, switch, conductor, insulator — through touch, talk, demonstration, and shared success. In the airplane-building challenge, students worked through roles and built something that had shape, motion, and purpose.

For future lessons, the goal is to design literacy tasks where students can show understanding through multiple modes: writing, speaking, drawing, recording, building, coding, peer teaching, and reflection. Critical questions also need to be built in so that digital literacy is also about understanding audience, representation, access, safety, and power.

Most importantly, these readings reinforced that students may already have rich digital and multimodal literacies — but those strengths only become visible when the classroom is designed to notice them.
