## 3 Quotes That Stood Out

**Quote 1 — Knobel & Lankshear**

> "New literacies are not simply new tools for old practices. They involve new ways of participating, new values, and new social relationships."

This line reframes the whole question of what literacy *is*. It is not enough to add a device, a screen, or a platform to a lesson and call it digital literacy. The participation structure, the values embedded in the task, and the relationships it creates all have to change too. In my IGNITE placement, I kept noticing that students weren't blocked by the concepts — they were blocked by access: login errors, multi-step interfaces, unclear click-paths, and routines that assumed digital fluency no one had actually taught. Knobel and Lankshear help me name that gap clearly: we were sometimes offering new tools for the same narrow participation structure, and that is not a new literacy.

**Quote 2 — Dr. Nichole Pinkard**

> "Digital literacy is an ecosystem — students need tools, mentors, audiences, feedback, and real reasons to create."

This one connected most directly to what I actually saw work in my placement. The Grade 4 Minecraft moment where students who finished early helped classmates who were still working — that was not accidental. It was an ecosystem in action: a student with a tool, a peer mentor, an audience (their classmate), feedback in real time, and a reason to create that went beyond finishing first. What Pinkard names theoretically, I watched happen in a fourth-grade classroom. What I want to carry forward is the intentional design of all five ecosystem elements, not just access to the device.

**Quote 3 — Melo-Pfeifer & Gertz**

> "Media messages do not only inform students — they teach students how to see people."

This is the line I will carry into every unit I ever design that uses media. As a Dominican student who grew up watching how my community was represented — or not represented, or misrepresented — in U.S. media, this is not abstract. The images, headlines, and stories students encounter teach them what is normal, who is dangerous, who is smart, and who belongs. Teaching students to read for what is absent and what is assumed is not a media studies add-on. It is a survival skill for students whose identities are at stake in those representations.

## 2 Questions

**Question 1**

Knobel and Lankshear argue that new literacies require new values and new social relationships — not just new tools. But in a school system where standards, assessments, and grade-level benchmarks still define literacy through print performance, how do teachers sustain new-literacies practices without those practices being flattened back into old forms the moment accountability pressure increases? Is this a pedagogical problem, a structural problem, or both?

**Question 2**

Melo-Pfeifer and Gertz focus on developing critical intercultural news literacy so students can question how minoritized communities are represented. But what do we do when the misrepresentation comes from inside the classroom — from a textbook, a curriculum resource, a district-approved platform, or a teacher's own blind spots? How do we teach students to read critically without inadvertently undermining their trust in school as a safe space, especially for students who already carry a lot of skepticism toward institutions?

## 1 Connection to Teaching Practice

In my Pine Brook IGNITE placement, I taught K–5 computer science and technology-integrated lessons across coding, robotics, Minecraft, digital design, and STEM challenges. One of the things I kept running into was the difference between engagement and access. Students could be excited about Minecraft and still not be able to participate fully — because they needed a login, a click-path, a vocabulary word, a peer model, or simply a first step that was visible and reachable. Pinkard's ecosystem model is the clearest framework I have found for naming what was missing in those moments: tools without mentors, tasks without authentic audiences, creation without feedback.

Module 3 connects those placement observations to a larger claim: digital literacy is identity work. Students do not enter digital spaces as blank users. They bring language, culture, family, community, histories, and bodies. Brooks and Frankel's work on transnational digital literacy describes students who may already be using technology to move across languages, countries, and communities — long before any teacher designs a lesson around it. In my placement, I saw this in students who switched between Spanish and English in their Minecraft chat, who drew on home knowledge to explain circuits, and who used gaming vocabulary to scaffold their understanding of computational concepts.

My commitment going forward is to design with Pinkard's ecosystem intentionally: not just providing tools, but building in mentors (including peer mentors), authentic audiences, feedback structures, and real reasons to create. And alongside that, I want to build in the critical dimension Melo-Pfeifer and Gertz describe — teaching students to ask not just *how does this work* but *who does this work for, who does it leave out, and what story does it tell about us*.
