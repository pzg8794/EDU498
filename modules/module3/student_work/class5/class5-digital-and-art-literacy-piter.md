## 1. In-Class Group Work — Digging into Readings

### Prompt 1: What kinds of meaning-making might we miss when looking only for traditional reading and writing?

**Diversity of stories:**  
We miss out on a diversity of stories. As of now, we mostly hear a very white-centered perspective of these stories. We should be engaging students with stories from different cultures and finding ways to embrace those stories in the classroom as three-dimensional.

**Student creativity in problem-solving:**  
Students can show digital literacy by using outside-the-box solutions to solve problems. In computer science, students read signs, words, symbols, patterns, and code to understand what is happening. Similarly, they build literacy by identifying patterns, testing possible solutions, and adjusting their thinking until they find a way forward. This shows that digital literacy is also about interpreting information, recognizing patterns, and creating solutions.

**Continuous feedback loop:**  
When students are playing video games, Minecraft, coding games, or similar activities, they can interact with the system, try different strategies, get feedback, and adjust what they are doing. They can also become experts while working with someone who is new to the activity, which helps both students grow. That process shows digital literacy because students are learning, revising, communicating, and building confidence through interaction.

### Prompt 2: How do we assess digital literacy without it being reduced to a written reflection or a test?

**Feedback loop:**  
Assess digital literacy by watching how students try something, get feedback from the tool, adjust, and try again. In coding games or similar activities, students read signs, patterns, directions, and code while troubleshooting problems. Peer support also counts because students explain their thinking and help others grow. This shows digital literacy as problem-solving, revision, communication, and learning in action.

**Deficiency focus:**  
Traditional literacy assessments often focus on what students cannot do or have not achieved yet. Digital literacy can help us assess students differently by identifying what they are already good at, comfortable with, or interested in. This gives students a place to start from and helps teachers build on their strengths instead of only measuring deficits.

**Strength-based assessment:**  
This fights back against the deficit model. Instead of only measuring what students lack, teachers can use students' strengths to assess what they have learned. That might include writing something down, creating an audio recording, creating artwork, explaining their thinking, or helping a peer.

### Prompt 3: How can I ensure I am considerate and respectful of my broader community and their values in the ways I introduce technology in my classroom, especially taking into consideration privacy concerns of vulnerable populations?

Culturally responsive technology use means designing access while protecting dignity. Technology should help students participate, create, and be seen, but not at the cost of their privacy, safety, or community values.

There is a rise in students who are immigrants, and teachers need to be mindful that some students may have to be careful about what personal information they share in class. Policies, cultural differences, family concerns, or safety issues may make sharing risky or uncomfortable for them.

It is important not to push students to share personal information. Instead, teachers should find creative ways for students to stay involved in the assignment while protecting their privacy, safety, and comfort.

The readings helped me see digital literacy from multiple angles: access, identity, critical thinking, and community. ## 3. Digital Literacy Recap — Group Response and Peer Connection

### Prompt: Digital Activism and Social Change

**Prompt text:** Examine the role of digital literacy in contemporary social movements. How have digital platforms, as discussed in the readings, been utilized for mobilization, education, or advocacy? Provide specific examples that illustrate these dynamics.

**Group response:**  
Digital platforms connect people from all over the world and can support movements such as Black Lives Matter, as well as public conversations about how Muslim communities were represented after 9/11 and how Asian communities were scapegoated during the COVID-19 pandemic. Digital tools can help classrooms and communities become more inclusive, especially for people who learn differently or need different ways to participate.

This connects to Stornaiuolo and Thomas because they show that digital spaces are not just places where young people consume information; they are also spaces where people organize, speak back, and challenge inequality. Black Lives Matter is a strong example because videos, hashtags, livestreams, and shared resources helped people document injustice, organize protests, educate others, and make systemic racism visible.

The readings also remind us that digital spaces can spread harm when people are not critically reading media. Melo-Pfeifer and Gertz's idea of critical intercultural news literacy connects to how Muslim communities were represented after 9/11 and how Asian communities were scapegoated during COVID.

Discussion forums can give participants a chance to reflect on social issues rather than become defensive immediately.

### Anonymous Peer Response We Connected To

A peer group discussed digital identity and self-expression on social media. They noted that social media profiles often look better than real life because people post in structured and curated ways. This can create stress, especially for teenagers or students comparing themselves to celebrities, successful people, or popular peers. They also explained that social media can constrain self-expression when students post based on what others want to see, but it can also enhance self-expression when students use it to connect with people and communities that matter to them. They connected this to Brooks and Frankel's discussion of refugee students using social media to maintain connections with people from their home countries.

### Piter's Connection

I connect with this because it shows that digital literacy is also about identity and self-representation. Social media can pressure students to perform a version of themselves that looks successful, perfect, or acceptable to others, but it can also become a space for connection and belonging. This connects to our discussion because we argued that digital literacy should help students think critically about representation, audience, safety, and power.

## 4. Art Literacy Deep Dive — Takeaway and Course Connection

One takeaway for me is that art literacy helps students understand that images are also forms of literature, just like texts. Students can read color, symbols, facial expressions, culture, and perspective to make meaning.

This connects directly to the discussions we have had through this module's readings because digital literacy is more than using technology. It is about interpreting messages, questioning representation, and noticing whose voices or stories are included or missing. Similar to digital media, art helps students think critically about identity, culture, power, and how meaning is created.

## 5. Art Literacy Activity — Selected Artwork

**Format:** Image  
**Artwork:** *Quilt, Crazy Pattern*  
**Artist:** Aletta Whitehouse Davis  
**Date:** ca. 1885  
**Museum:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art  

I chose *Quilt, Crazy Pattern* because it visually represents my Puzzle Plan, a research framework that brings together multiple literatures through multiple identities. The quilt is made of many fragments: different fabrics, colors, textures, patterns, and shapes. At first, it can look chaotic, but when you take a step back, the pieces form one connected design. This artwork connects to how I understand literacy and learning, especially in teaching. Students bring different identities, cultures, languages, bodies, histories, access needs, and ways of making meaning. Those pieces may look disconnected if we look at them only through traditional ways of assessing literacy. However, through the lenses of my Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS, an equity-based research framework, they become part of a larger system of access, identity, joy, criticality, and transformation.

Furthermore, this connects to the Art Literacy theory deep dive because art can be read in the same manner we read text. The quilt tells a story through visual fragments instead of written words. It forces us to notice patterns, relationships, contrast, and meaning. Personally, this artwork opens a discussion about how schools often treat students' differences as scattered or extra, when in reality those differences are the material of the whole design: the whole person.

## Course Theme Connection

This class work continues the EDU498 theme that literacy is not only reading and writing. Digital literacy and art literacy both ask us to read systems, images, tools, identities, histories, power, and representation. Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS lens, the work shows that students' fragments are not deficits. They are parts of a whole person, and the classroom has to be designed so those pieces can be recognized, protected, and valued.
