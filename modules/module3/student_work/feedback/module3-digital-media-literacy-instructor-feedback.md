## Specific Teaching Feedback

The instructor responded especially to the second question:

> How do we teach critical media literacy to young students in a way that is honest, not overwhelming?

The key feedback was that critical inquiry should become a classroom norm, not an occasional add-on. Questions like "Who is missing?" and "What story is being repeated?" can become starters for whole-class discussion.

The instructor suggested several discussion formats for this kind of work:

- debate,
- discussion,
- circle talk,
- selected-topic conversations.

The most important takeaway was that prioritizing critical conversations can drive engagement instead of instructional compliance. In other words, students are more likely to participate meaningfully when they are invited to question, interpret, and discuss, rather than only complete fill-in-the-blank style answers.

## Follow-Up Moves to Consider

### 1. Turn critical questions into classroom routines

Instead of saving critical media questions for the end of a lesson, build them into the structure of the activity. For example:

- Who is included?
- Who is missing?
- What story is being repeated?
- What assumptions are being made?
- Who benefits from this message?
- Who might be harmed by this message?

### 2. Use discussion formats intentionally

For younger students, critical inquiry can be supported through low-pressure structures such as circle talk, turn-and-talk, visual noticing routines, or guided whole-class discussion. This can make critical media literacy honest without making it overwhelming.

### 3. Connect critical inquiry to joy and access

Criticality does not have to remove joy from the classroom. It can deepen joy by giving students voice, dignity, and a real reason to participate. This fits the course theme because students are not just completing tasks; they are learning how to read the world and speak back to it.

### 4. Apply this to future digital, art, and AI literacy lessons

This feedback should carry forward into later lessons. Whether students are reading images, using digital tools, building with AI, or creating multimodal projects, the classroom should include questions that help students think about audience, representation, power, privacy, safety, and belonging.

## Puzzle Plan / EQUITAS Connection

This feedback strengthens the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS direction because it confirms that critical inquiry belongs inside the design of the lesson. Access is not only about tools or formats. Access also means creating classroom structures where students can question, interpret, disagree respectfully, and connect their own perspectives to larger social issues.

For future teaching, the goal is to design lessons where critical questions are part of the pathway into learning, not an extra reflection after the real work is finished.
