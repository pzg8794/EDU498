## Puzzle Plan And EQUITAS Connection

### Access

Art literacy creates multiple entry points for students who may not show their strongest thinking through traditional print-only assignments. Students can interpret images, notice symbols, respond emotionally, connect art to history, or create visual arguments.

### Identity

The video repeatedly shows how art makes identity visible. Wiley, Marshall, Greene, Ali-Coleman, Alderman, and Rivera are used to show that art can challenge whose bodies, histories, and communities are centered.

### Healing

Art can give students a way to process grief, trauma, historical violence, public memory, and personal meaning without reducing them to a single written response. This matters for students navigating trauma, depression, chronic illness, disability, or other overlapping conditions.

### Agency

Art literacy positions students as interpreters, critics, creators, and cultural meaning-makers. They are not only consuming art; they are learning how to make claims about the world through visual evidence.

### Criticality

The artwork examples ask students to question war, racism, erasure, charity, capitalism, public memory, and exclusion. Art literacy becomes a way to read power.

### Joy

Art literacy also makes room for wonder, beauty, curiosity, and emotional connection. Joy appears through interpretation, shared looking, cultural recognition, and the feeling that students' responses matter.

### Transformation

The video supports a broader definition of literacy that includes visual interpretation, cultural analysis, civic engagement, public memory, and multimodal expression. In the EDU498 frame, art literacy helps move difference from deficit to brilliance.

## Teaching Implications

- Use artwork as a text, not as decoration.
- Invite multiple interpretations before giving a single expert reading.
- Pair visual analysis with identity, culture, history, and power.
- Let students respond through drawing, discussion, writing, audio, movement, annotation, or digital composition.
- Treat art literacy as rigorous critical literacy, especially for students who need multimodal access.
- Use public art and community art to connect literacy learning to place, memory, and civic life.
- Ask whose stories are visible, whose are missing, and what the artwork asks viewers to do.

## Possible Discussion Takeaway

Art literacy matters because it teaches students to read the world visually, culturally, emotionally, and politically. For neurodivergent, CLD, multilingual, disabled, trauma-impacted, and multiply identified students, this expands literacy beyond a narrow print-based model and creates more ways to participate with power, joy, and critical agency.
