## Study Transcript

Digital citizenship teaches students how to participate in online spaces with care, responsibility, safety, and critical awareness. It is more than a set of internet rules. It is part of digital literacy because students are constantly reading, interpreting, creating, and responding to digital texts such as websites, videos, posts, images, comments, search results, messages, and online communities.

A digital citizenship lesson asks students to think about who they are online, how their choices affect others, and how technology shapes identity, learning, relationships, and community. Students need support with privacy, digital footprints, online communication, media balance, misinformation, respectful participation, and safe decision-making.

For EDU498, this connects to culturally and historically responsive literacy because digital spaces are not neutral. Students enter them with different access, languages, identities, experiences, and levels of support. A strong lesson should not assume that every student has the same device access, confidence, background knowledge, or emotional safety online.

Through the Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS lens, digital citizenship becomes a question of access and agency. Teachers should ask who is protected online, who is misrepresented, who is excluded, and what supports students need to participate meaningfully. Students should learn to question digital media by asking who created a message, what purpose it serves, who is represented, who is missing, and what assumptions are being repeated.

The goal is not only to help students avoid harm. The goal is to help students become thoughtful, critical, caring, and empowered digital participants. Digital citizenship should help students use technology to learn, create, collaborate, protect themselves, support others, and challenge unfair or harmful narratives.

## EDU498 Teaching Connection

In future teaching, digital citizenship should be taught as literacy practice. Students can show understanding through discussion, writing, drawing, video, role-play, peer teaching, or digital creation. This connects to the Grade 4 Minecraft placement example, where students who finished early helped classmates continue their work. That moment showed digital literacy as community responsibility, not just individual task completion.

---

## Key Takeaway

Digital citizenship is literacy because it teaches students how to read digital spaces, understand their responsibilities, protect themselves and others, and participate with care, critical thinking, and agency.
