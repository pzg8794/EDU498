## Part 1 - Building on the Basics

One thing I think is still missing from the New Basics is a stronger commitment to literacy as **critical participation**, especially in digital and multimodal spaces. The New Basics move us beyond the old idea that literacy is only print reading and writing, but I think they still need to name more directly that students already come to school with real literacies from their homes, communities, cultures, languages, technologies, and interests. In my Pine Brook IGNITE placement, I have seen elementary students use literacy while debugging block code, navigating Chromebooks and Google Classroom, interpreting icons and digital interfaces, building in Minecraft, using AI as a learning helper, and giving classmates "glows and grows" on their projects. Those are not extra skills outside literacy. They are ways students read, compose, collaborate, explain, revise, and participate.

The Culturally and Historically Responsive foundation I would add as a basic is **criticality**. Students need literacy practices that help them question power, access, representation, and whose voices are centered or erased. This feels especially important in computer science and digital literacy, where AI tools, algorithms, platforms, and school devices can look neutral while still shaping privacy, authorship, access, and belonging. Bishop's mirrors, windows, and sliding glass doors also helps me think about this because students need texts and literacy practices that reflect them, help them understand others, and let them enter new worlds without being asked to leave themselves behind. Mackey's question of who reads what, in what formats, and why pushes me to see literacy as purpose-driven and socially situated, not just a set of school skills. A fuller version of the New Basics would treat students as multimodal meaning-makers who can read, write, speak, listen, code, design, critique, and create across many contexts.

## Part 2 - New Learners and New Teachers

What feels missing from the New Teachers definitions is the idea that teachers should design learning environments **with** students, families, communities, and colleagues, not only **for** students. The definitions name collaboration and flexible pathways, but I think they need to go further in describing teachers as listeners, interpreters, co-learners, and designers who study the conditions around learning before judging the learner. My own teaching philosophy has been shaped by the idea that equity requires design, not just good intentions. In placement, I have learned that a student who struggles with a multi-step digital task, a noisy room, a new interface, or written directions may still be showing strong thinking. For example, in IGNITE I have seen students get stuck on the click-path into a tool or the sequence of digital steps while still explaining their choices, helping a peer debug, or revising their product after feedback. Difficulty with access is not the same thing as lack of intellect.

The Culturally and Historically Responsive teaching approach I would make central to New Teachers is **intellect**. New Teachers should offer rigorous learning that builds deep knowledge, not watered-down tasks, while also removing unnecessary barriers through modeling, visual supports, chunked directions, partner talk, flexible ways to show learning, and clear success criteria. In my placement work, I keep returning to structures like "plan -> build in chunks -> validate and revise," student choice in topics and products, peer feedback through glows and grows, and short proof tasks where students explain, show, save, or demonstrate what they learned. These moves position students as capable knowledge-makers rather than passive recipients of curriculum. Ultimately, the classroom I want to build is one where students are invited to bring their full literacies into rigorous work and leave more convinced that their minds matter.
