# Class 4 – Asynchronous Journal
## EDU498 | Piter Garcia Bautista
### Prompt: Racial Literacy, Identity, and the "Inconsistent Student" Trope

---

## Describe the Moment

As a Latino (Dominican) male teenager, I repeatedly experienced moments where I felt that education expected me to perform at my peak consistently and without visible struggle. I was expected to sit still, focus on command, memorize deadlines, regulate my emotions like a “man” without professional help, complete assignments at a pace above everyone else, showcasing my abilities through neat and predictable academic behavior, especially through my paintings. However, because of how my brain works, I often lived in extremes. I could become deeply focused, creative, and analytical; the top student everyone expected. But, when my brain or body was overwhelmed, I could look like the opposite: distracted, late, inconsistent, unmotivated, or behind. The painful part was, and still is, that the same system that praised me at my peak often judged me when I struggled. Moreover, as a Latino man, admitting my struggles was a sign of weakness. Because of that, I was ashamed of that side and hid it, thinking it was my broken side, failing to recognize that both versions were me.

---

## Excavate the Moment

The belief reinforced was that a “smart” male student should be consistently high-performing, organized, emotionally regulated, and easy to teach. If I was capable most days but struggled on some, the assumption was often that I was lazy, careless, or not trying hard enough. This was the same stereotype I experienced when I was chronically ill: everything was “in my head” until the day I started bleeding inside. That belief comes from a larger culture that treats consistency as proof of intelligence and macho endurance as proof of a man’s character. This comes from deficit-based views of disability, masculinity, and neurodivergence, where students are judged by how they match the “normal” learner and not by how much work they are doing to participate. Historically and culturally, schools have often been built around narrow ideas of literacy, ability, behavior, and success. As students, we are expected to show learning in standardized ways, speeds, and forms of attention or communication. This leaves little room for students whose brains, bodies, cultures, languages, trauma, or identities shape learning differently. For me, the stereotype underneath was: intelligence should come naturally, without working hard or showing “weakness.” As a Latino man, neurodivergence was not a thing, so if I showed a sign of struggle, something was wrong with me — erasing the reality that I worked harder than most.

---

## Reflect on the Impact of the Moment

At the time, this affected me deeply; it made me question myself. I knew I was capable, but I could not always access my abilities on demand, which created shame, confusion, anxiety, and exhaustion that I still have to navigate as a student. I felt the constant pressure of proving I belonged by overperforming. If I did not perform at my highest level, I feared people would judge the struggling version of me. This created a painful split in how I understood myself. I felt I was either the best of the best or the worst of the worst, with very little room to simply be human. Both outcomes were connected to the same reality: I was navigating school with a neurodivergent brain, stress, and invisible barriers that were not always recognized. Looking back, the emotions are different but strong. I feel sadness for the younger version of myself who thought inconsistency meant failure, making health sacrifices that I now have to reconcile with. I feel anger at systems that interpret access needs as character flaws. Most importantly, I feel compassion and pride because I can now see how much work I was doing. I was not broken; I was surviving a system that did not know how to read me.

---

## Interrupt the Moment

What I understand now is that the trope of the “inconsistent student” is dangerous. A student who seems brilliant one day and struggles the next is not necessarily careless or lazy. They may be neurodivergent, depressed, ill, or carrying trauma, masking that “broken” side with a smile, while using enormous energy just to participate. To interrupt this belief, I have stopped reading my own fluctuations as moral failure, and remember that my worth is not measured by whether I can perform at my peak every day. My brain’s intensity, creativity, and pattern recognition are strengths, and the challenges around energy and consistency are part of how I experience and process the world differently. To interrupt this belief in my circles, I speak honestly and openly about how neurodivergence, depression, chronic illness, and trauma affect learning. I challenge language that labels CLD students, especially Latinos, without asking what support they need. And, I advocate for understanding that access is what makes participation meaningful and possible.

In my teaching practice, interrupting this trope means refusing to judge students only by visible behavior or work. It means designing classrooms where students have multiple ways to show understanding, where inconsistency is treated as information and not failure, and relationships that help teachers notice what students are carrying. It means remembering that literacy is not only reading and writing, but also decoding expectations, navigating systems, translating identity, and surviving spaces that were not built with them in mind. The commitment I carry forward is to build learning environments where students do not have to become less themselves in order to be seen as capable.
