## 📚 Assigned Readings & Media

### 1. *Unearthing Joy* — Gholdy Muhammad (Referenced throughout)
> **Core argument:** Joy is not a supplement to rigorous literacy instruction — it is a pillar of it. Muhammad frames joy as one of five pursuits in Culturally and Historically Responsive Education (CHRE): identity, skills, intellectualism, criticality, and **joy**. She argues that Black and Brown students have historically been denied joy in schooling, and that reclaiming it is an act of justice and resistance.

**Connection to our theme:**  
Muhammad’s framework makes joy a *designed* condition, not an accidental outcome. This directly supports the idea that joy requires institutional recognition — if it is not built into curriculum design, assessment, and pedagogy, it is not accessible. A student who codes, creates, or debugs may be experiencing genuine joyful literacy, but if the institution does not name it as such, it disappears from the record.

---

### 2. “Unfolding joy in young children’s literacy practices in a Finnish early years classroom”
> **Core argument:** Joy in literacy is visible and embodied — a child finishing a handmade storybook and hugging it to his chest is joy. The article identifies joy as student-centered, unfolding through authentic creation and choice, not through teacher-directed compliance.

**Connection to our theme:**  
The Finnish classroom example shows joy emerging when structure *allows* for authentic expression. The key variable is not just student motivation but classroom design. The teacher created accessible conditions (choice, time, materials, low-stakes creation) that made joy possible. Without accessible structures, that same child may have never finished the book — or would have had his joy go unseen.

**Referenced by classmate:** Christina cited this article directly — the hugging-the-book moment.

### 3. Readings on Joy, Justice, and Culturally Responsive Practice (General thread across class)
> **Core argument (synthesized):** Joyful literacy has historical roots dating back to the 1700s but has been largely suppressed by standardized curriculum models. Joy is not just happiness — it is a revolutionary, justice-linked stance. Joy connected to race, culture, and identity is not optional enrichment; it is the foundation of meaningful literacy engagement for historically marginalized students.

**Connection to our theme:**  
Joy linked to justice (Anvitha’s observation) and joy as more than happiness (Zoe’s observation) both land in the same place: joy that is not institutionally *accessible* — that is gated by curriculum format, language, or assessment mode — is not actually available to all students. A student who speaks Spanish at home, thinks in code, or learns through images and diagrams is capable of deep joyful literacy. But institutional recognition must expand to meet them.

**Key instructor framing from slides:**
> *Joy can and should be an educational pursuit, where we ask ourselves:*
> - How does my curriculum and instruction **enable, amplify, and spread** joy?
> - What types of joy could I **bring into** the curriculum?
> - How does my curriculum **elevate beauty within humanity**?
> - How will I write **learning objectives** that help students pursue and embrace joy?
> - How will I **know** if my students are experiencing joy?

This framing is structurally important: the instructor is asking us to *operationalize* joy — to make it visible, measurable, and designed. That is recognition and accessibility built into pedagogical planning.
