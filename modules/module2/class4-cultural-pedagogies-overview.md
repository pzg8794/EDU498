# Class 4: Cultural Pedagogies — Full Prep Guide

**Course:** EDU498 · Literacy Learning as Social Practice
**Theme:** Cultural pedagogy is not about adding culture to school. It is about redesigning school so students' languages, histories, identities, literacies, and community practices are sustained as legitimate knowledge.
**Personal throughline:** Joy requires recognition and accessibility. Class 3 named joy as something that must be designed for. Class 4 asks whose culture, language, history, and way of knowing gets recognized enough for joy and belonging to become possible.

## Readings & Media

### 1. Muhammad, G. (2020). *Cultivating Genius*, Chapter 2 (pp. 38–61)
*(Required textbook — not linked; access via RIT library or your copy)*

**Core argument**
Muhammad argues that literacy instruction must go beyond skills, standards, and test preparation. Literacy is tied to identity, knowledge, power, self-definition, and liberation. Her Culturally and Historically Responsive Literacy (CHRL) framework is built around four major pursuits:

- **Identity** — learning about oneself and others
- **Skills** — developing academic and disciplinary abilities
- **Intellect** — building knowledge about the world
- **Criticality** — understanding and disrupting power, oppression, and inequity

This course adds **joy** alongside these four, which fits the direction of Muhammad's later work and the Class 3 focus.

**What this chapter does for Class 4**
Chapter 2 grounds the move from general "culturally responsive teaching" toward culturally and historically responsive *literacy*. Culture is not surface-level classroom decoration — not just food, holidays, or "diverse books." Culture is tied to historical memory, community knowledge, language practices, identity formation, and intellectual traditions. Students should not have to leave their identities at the classroom door to be seen as literate.

**Key question Muhammad helps answer**
> What is literacy *for*?
Not only: Can students decode? Can they write a paragraph?
But also: Can students understand themselves? Build knowledge? Critique injustice? Use literacy for agency, healing, joy, and action?

**Classroom implication**
A Muhammad-style lesson asks not only *what standard am I teaching?* but also *what identity work, knowledge-building, critical thinking, and joy are built into this lesson?*

**Connection to theme**
Muhammad gives the academic framework for the claim: joy cannot be separated from identity, intellect, skills, and criticality. If a student's culture, language, or way of thinking is not recognized, the classroom may demand literacy while denying belonging.

### 2. Paris, D., & Alim, H. S. (2014). What are we seeking to sustain through culturally sustaining pedagogy? A loving critique forward. *Harvard Educational Review, 84*(1), 85–100.
[📄 Access PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1o0qw4lwemKv8D0yXugwkLGadHPv_r_xs/view?usp=share_link)

**Core argument**
Paris and Alim argue that education needs to move beyond being merely culturally relevant or asset-based. Culturally Sustaining Pedagogy (CSP) aims to *sustain* students' linguistic, literate, and cultural practices — rather than using them only as stepping stones toward dominant school norms. CSP supports linguistic, literate, and cultural pluralism as part of democratic schooling.

**The "loving critique"**
The article calls itself a loving critique because it respects earlier asset pedagogies while arguing the field must go further. It critiques three things:
- Earlier asset pedagogies that valued but did not *sustain* students' cultures
- Approaches focused only on heritage culture without recognizing contemporary youth culture
- Approaches that celebrated youth culture without critically examining inequalities within it

**White gaze and schooling**
Schooling has too often measured students of color against White, middle-class norms of language, behavior, and achievement. Instead of asking how closely students approximate dominant norms, CSP asks: what would pedagogy look like if schools *explored, honored, extended, and sometimes problematized* students' actual community practices?

**Moving from "relevant" to "sustaining"**
- *Culturally relevant pedagogy* asks: How do we make learning meaningful in relation to students' cultures?
- *Culturally sustaining pedagogy* asks: How do we ensure students' cultures, languages, and literacies *continue to live, grow, and matter* inside school?

Something can be "relevant" without being sustained — a teacher might reference Hip Hop or Black language to hook engagement but still return to "real" academic English. CSP pushes against that.

**Classroom implications**
- Do not use students' cultures only as hooks
- Do not treat home language as a bridge to "proper" language only
- Include contemporary youth literacies: music, digital media, games, memes, social platforms, oral language, remix, coding, and design
- Teach dominant codes without making them the only legitimate form of intelligence
- Let students critique culture — including their own communities — with care

**Connection to theme**
Students cannot fully belong if school only recognizes their culture as useful when it helps them move toward dominant norms. For the mini-unit: Minecraft, coding, design, and peer explanation are literacies worth *sustaining*, not "fun extras."

### 3. Strekalova-Hughes, E., & Wang, X. C. (2019). Perspectives of children from refugee backgrounds on their family storytelling as a culturally sustaining practice. *Journal of Research in Childhood Education, 33*(1), 6–21.
[📄 Access PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1OJcSDtC-1AKCthFn3ge9xgRnxECZR0ll/view?usp=share_link)

**Core argument**
Children from refugee backgrounds (Nepali, Somali, South Sudanese families in western New York) understand family storytelling as a culturally sustaining practice. Family storytelling is not just a home activity — it is a form of intergenerational literacy, cultural memory, language maintenance, identity formation, and community knowledge.

**Why this matters**
Refugee-background students are frequently described through trauma, vulnerability, or lack. The authors center children's voices instead, asking what they value about their own family storytelling. The article shows that children from refugee backgrounds may be pressured by school to abandon their languages, literacies, and cultures in order to succeed.

**Theoretical frameworks**
- **Culturally Sustaining Pedagogy** — used to ask whether family storytelling is a practice children want to sustain, and how schools might learn from it
- **Refugee Critical Race Theory (RefugeeCrit)** — foregrounds children's and families' agency; challenges the reduction of refugee stories to narratives of persecution, need, or charity

**Key findings — what children noticed and valued**
- Repeated storytelling and remembering
- Narrative structure, gestures, and performance
- Cultural lessons and family connection
- Language learning
- Similarities and differences between home and school storytelling
- Concern about losing native languages
- Awareness that peers may lack the cultural context to fully understand family stories

**Classroom implications**
- Treat family storytelling as literacy
- Invite home/community stories without forcing students to share trauma
- Build cultural context before asking students to interpret stories
- Value oral, embodied, repeated, and multilingual storytelling
- Recognize children as experts on their own family practices
- Avoid turning refugee students into "case studies" of suffering

**Connection to theme**
Students cannot build meaning from a text or lesson if the classroom gives them no context, no recognition, and no pathway in. The article shows that children already have rich literacy practices at home. The school's job is not to replace those practices with school literacy but to recognize and sustain them. *Family storytelling becomes joyful literacy when schools recognize it as knowledge, not as background information.*

### 4. Ladson-Billings, G. (2021). Three decades of culturally relevant, responsive, & sustaining pedagogy: What lies ahead? *The Educational Forum, 85*(4), 351–354.
[📄 Access PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/10GLEgmaJtoUi7hPJ7y3S1-9s4POAALTZ/view?usp=share_link)

**Core argument**
Ladson-Billings reflects on the history and future of Culturally Relevant Pedagogy (CRP). CRP is not a vague feel-good approach — it has three core commitments:
1. Advancing student learning
2. Developing cultural competence
3. Fostering critical consciousness

Teaching only counts as culturally relevant if it remains faithful to all three.

**Warning against watered-down versions**
Many top-down school/district efforts fall short of the theory's original design. Workshops and certifications may promise to "make" teachers culturally relevant, but they often become prescriptive and disconnected from deeper theory. Culturally responsive teaching is not a checklist. "Include diverse books," "celebrate heritage month," and "build relationships" may matter — but they are not enough.

**Pandemic reset**
Ladson-Billings writes in the context of overlapping crises: COVID-19, systemic racism, economic collapse, and environmental catastrophe. These crises exposed how schools were already failing many students before the pandemic. She strongly rejects returning to "normal" — for many students, normal already meant academic failure, exclusion, and lack of support.

**What she wants instead**
A reset toward more integrated, problem-based, creative, and student-centered approaches. Students are already creating, communicating, producing, building businesses, using digital tools, and mastering platforms. The mismatch between 21st-century students and 19th-century schooling is not acceptable.

**Classroom implications**
- Do not reduce CRP/CSP to a checklist
- Do not mistake representation for transformation
- Design instruction that cultivates student voice, agency, creativity, and critical consciousness
- Treat STEM, coding, digital design, and problem-based learning as valid spaces for culturally sustaining work
- Use frameworks as guidance, not scripts

**Connection to theme**
Students were already showing genius, creativity, and agency — but schools often failed to recognize it. Students debug, design, explain, build, help peers, interpret visuals — but if school only counts worksheets and tests, their literacy becomes invisible.

### 5. Gorman, A. (2021, January 20). *The Hill We Climb* [Video]. The Guardian.
[▶ Watch / Read Transcript](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2021/jan/20/amanda-gorman-poem-biden-inauguration-transcript) (5:43)

**Core argument**
Gorman's inaugural poem speaks from national grief toward democratic repair, collective responsibility, and the possibility of building something better — without pretending harm did not happen. The nation is framed as unfinished: something people must keep composing, repairing, and reimagining. She calls for a country committed *across cultures, colors, characters, and conditions.*

**Why it belongs in Class 4**
This poem is a cultural text about identity, history, race, democracy, and repair. Poetry here is not private emotion only — it is civic literacy. Gorman refuses the idea of returning to the past. Repair requires moving toward what *should be*, not simply restoring what *was*. This directly echoes Ladson-Billings's argument that schools should not rush back to a "normal" that was already failing many students.

**Strong classroom discussion questions**
- Who is included in Gorman's "we," and who has historically been excluded from that "we"?
- What does it mean to "repair" a country, a classroom, or a curriculum?

**Classroom implications**
- Close reading and spoken-word analysis
- Civic and racial literacy
- Historical memory and discussion of democracy
- Multimodal performance and student writing about repair, belonging, and collective responsibility

**Connection to theme**
Gorman treats hope as a demand, not a decoration. The poem does not say joy means ignoring harm. It says hope requires courage, action, and shared responsibility — which fits the framing: *joy connected to justice is not just celebration; it is a demand.*

### 6. Sealey-Ruiz, Y. (2022). *Truth, Love & Racial Literacy* [Video]. YouTube.
[▶ Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-V_GdpVR6NI)

**Core argument**
Racial literacy is not only knowing facts about racism. It is the ability to read, name, interpret, and respond to race and racism in oneself, institutions, texts, curriculum, relationships, and classroom interactions. Teachers cannot do culturally responsive or sustaining work without doing internal work first.

**Key concept: Archaeology of Self**
A reflective process where educators examine their own histories, biases, assumptions, racial socialization, wounds, privileges, fears, and inherited beliefs — not for guilt's sake, but for honesty. Teachers must ask:
- What did I learn about race, intelligence, and language growing up?
- What kinds of student behavior do I read as "disrespectful" or "off-task"?
- Whose knowledge do I trust quickly? Whose requires extra proof?
- What do I call "academic," and what do I dismiss?

**Truth and love**
- **Truth** means refusing to hide from racism, inequity, harm, and institutional history
- **Love** does not mean niceness or avoiding hard topics — it means committing to students' humanity enough to teach truthfully and protect dignity

**Classroom implications**
- Do self-reflection before designing curriculum
- Do not ask students of color to educate the class about racism
- Do not use love as an excuse to avoid truth
- Build racial literacy into everyday classroom practice, not only crisis moments
- Recognize that teacher interpretation is never neutral

**Connection to theme**
Recognition is not automatic. Teachers have to learn how to recognize students' thinking, language, culture, and joy without filtering everything through deficit assumptions. *Belonging before building also applies to the teacher: the teacher must examine what they are prepared to recognize before students can safely show what they know.*

## Cross-Reading Themes for Discussion

### Culture is not an add-on
Across Muhammad, Paris & Alim, Strekalova-Hughes & Wang, and Ladson-Billings, culture is not classroom decoration. It is part of how students know, read, speak, remember, relate, and build meaning. → *Culture must be structurally recognized, not symbolically included.*

### Access does not mean assimilation
Paris and Alim are especially clear: access should not mean "help students get closer to White middle-class norms." Students still need access to dominant codes, but equity is better pursued by centering students' heritage and contemporary practices. → *Joy is not accessible if students must abandon themselves to participate.*

### Students already bring literacy
Strekalova-Hughes and Wang show that children from refugee backgrounds already participate in complex literacy through family storytelling. Ladson-Billings similarly emphasizes that students are already creative, entrepreneurial, digital, social, and intellectually active. → *The question is not whether students are literate. The question is whether school knows how to see their literacies.*

### Pedagogy requires critique
These readings do not ask teachers to celebrate everything uncritically. Paris and Alim note youth culture can resist dominant power but can also reproduce inequality. Sealey-Ruiz adds that teachers must examine their own racial assumptions. Gorman's poem holds hope and critique together. → *Joy without critique becomes comfort. Joy with critique becomes justice.*

### Frameworks are not scripts
Ladson-Billings warns against turning CRP/CSP into checklists, workshops, or certifications. These frameworks require teacher judgment, humility, creativity, and sustained practice. → *Belonging before building is not a strategy to copy. It is a design commitment.*

## Best Discussion-Ready Synthesis

Across the Class 4 readings, cultural pedagogy is not presented as simply making school more inclusive or adding diverse materials. Muhammad, Paris and Alim, Strekalova-Hughes and Wang, Ladson-Billings, Gorman, and Sealey-Ruiz all push toward a deeper claim: students' identities, languages, histories, family practices, and ways of knowing must be sustained as real sources of knowledge. This connects to the developing theme that joy requires recognition and accessibility. If schools only recognize dominant forms of literacy, then students may be participating, thinking, designing, storytelling, translating, or building meaning in ways the institution fails to see. Culturally sustaining pedagogy asks teachers to redesign the conditions of learning so students do not have to leave themselves behind in order to succeed.

## Discussion Questions to Bring to Class

1. What is the difference between using culture as a bridge and sustaining culture as a goal?
2. How can teachers give students access to dominant academic skills without treating dominant norms as superior?
3. What counts as evidence of literacy if we take family storytelling, digital design, peer teaching, translanguaging, and youth culture seriously?
4. How can teachers invite students' cultural knowledge without asking students to perform identity or trauma for the classroom?
5. What does "belonging before building" look like in culturally sustaining pedagogy?
6. How does racial literacy change what teachers notice, value, and assess?
