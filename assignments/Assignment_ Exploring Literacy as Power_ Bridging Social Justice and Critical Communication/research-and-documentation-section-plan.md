## Our Social Justice Topic

**Communication access injustice for neurodivergent and chronically ill students in STEM education.**

This topic fits the assignment because it sits directly at the intersection of literacy and social justice. Literacy is not treated only as reading and writing. In this paper, literacy includes the ability to access, decode, interpret, and respond to classroom communication systems.

In STEM courses, those systems include:

- syllabus language;
- assignment instructions;
- grading policies;
- verbal-only updates;
- accommodation procedures;
- emails;
- peer evaluation expectations;
- team communication platforms;
- scientific terminology;
- code/documentation norms;
- institutional support pathways.

When these systems are unclear, inconsistent, verbal-only, inaccessible, or designed around one default learner, they can exclude students whose brains, bodies, languages, trauma histories, or health conditions require different communication supports.

## Working Definition

Communication access injustice occurs when educational systems require students to understand, remember, interpret, and act on classroom expectations through communication formats that are not equally accessible.

In this paper, that includes situations where:

- graded expectations are announced verbally but not written clearly;
- students are expected to infer hidden rules;
- accommodation language becomes a limit or warning instead of a design support;
- health disclosure shifts responsibility back onto the sick student;
- scientific jargon is assumed rather than taught;
- team communication tools exist without shared norms;
- students must repeatedly translate their disability, illness, or confusion into institutional language before they are believed;
- a student's need for clarity is misread as difficulty, irresponsibility, or resistance.

## Historically Relevant Social Justice Connection

The topic is historically relevant because schools have often organized learning around a narrow image of the "successful" student: someone who can sit still, remember oral instructions, decode institutional expectations, use dominant academic language, respond quickly, communicate calmly under stress, and perform knowledge in approved formats.

Students who do not match that image have often been misread. Neurodivergent students may be seen as careless, disruptive, lazy, too intense, or disorganized. Chronically ill students may be seen as unreliable. CLD and multilingual students may be seen as less prepared when the real issue is institutional language. Students with trauma, depression, or anxiety may be seen as avoidant or difficult when the communication environment feels threatening.

The issue is especially urgent in STEM because STEM classrooms can confuse rigor with rigidity. A course can be intellectually demanding without being inaccessible. But when instructors treat ambiguity, verbal-only memory, single-mode output, or emotional toughness as part of rigor, students with access needs are quietly sorted out.

## Field and Local Community Connection

This issue belongs to Piter's field and local educational community in multiple ways.

### Field of Study: Bioinformatics / Computer Science / STEM

The BIO/STEM evidence case sits in a bioinformatics context where students were expected to navigate scientific language, code, data files, report formats, peer evaluation, team workflows, and instructor expectations. That makes the case especially relevant to STEM literacy.

In this context, literacy includes:

- reading scientific instructions;
- interpreting computational tasks;
- understanding file formats and reference materials;
- communicating through code and documentation;
- coordinating team work through digital tools;
- translating technical understanding into graded submissions;
- navigating the institutional language of accommodations and grading.

### Local Community: Higher Education and Teacher Preparation

The local community connection is EDU498 and Piter's teaching-placement work. The adult BIO/STEM case shows what happens when inaccessible communication harms a student with advocacy tools. The teaching-placement connection shows why this matters even more for children.

Children often cannot document harm, email advisors, cite accommodations, or explain that a classroom expectation is inaccessible by design. They may simply become overwhelmed, shut down, act out, or internalize the message that they are the problem.

## Why This Issue Is Important to Draw Attention To

This issue is important because inaccessible communication can break students while still appearing professionally reasonable on the surface. A teacher may seem kind, organized, knowledgeable, or approachable, yet still create conditions that exclude students through unclear language, hidden rules, public pressure, and one-size-fits-all expectations.

The harm is serious because it can affect:

- physical health;
- executive functioning;
- mental health;
- peer relationships;
- academic confidence;
- trust in teachers;
- willingness to seek help;
- identity as a capable learner;
- future participation in STEM.

The most important reason to draw attention to this issue is protection. If an adult graduate student with documentation habits, technical expertise, advisor support, and self-advocacy skills can still be harmed by inaccessible communication, then children and less-resourced students are at even greater risk.
