# Journal Writing Process

## Assignment

**Literacy as Power: Bridging Social Justice and Critical Communication**

## Purpose of This Process Document

This document captures the working process for developing the final journal/paper. The goal is to preserve the reasoning path before drafting so the final submission stays grounded in the assignment requirements, the evidence record, EDU498 course frameworks, and Piter's larger Puzzle Plan/EQUITAS purpose.

This process exists so the journal does not become a rushed emotional response or a disconnected academic paper. It should become a bridge: from documented harm, to critical literacy analysis, to social justice recommendations that protect neurodivergent and chronically ill students, especially younger students who cannot defend themselves in the same way.

## Core Claim We Are Building Toward

Literacy is power because communication determines access. In STEM classrooms, the language of requirements, deadlines, grading policies, accommodations, team roles, feedback, and disciplinary expectations can either make learning possible or quietly exclude students who do not match the assumed default learner.

For neurodivergent and chronically ill students, communication failures are not minor inconveniences. Vague instructions, verbal-only rules, hidden grading expectations, single-mode assignments, public questioning, and unclear accommodation pathways can trigger health conditions, worsen anxiety, damage peer relationships, and make students appear careless or difficult when the actual problem is inaccessible design.

## Moral Center

The emotional center of the journal is:

> If this happened to an adult neurodivergent, chronically ill graduate student with documentation habits, technical expertise, advisor support, and the ability to advocate, what happens to children and younger students who cannot defend themselves yet?

This question should guide the entire draft. The paper is not about revenge. It is about protection, redesign, and justice.

## Working Identity of the Journal

The final journal should be a **critical journal of witness and design**.

- **Witness:** It preserves what happened and how it felt.
- **Evidence:** It roots claims in emails, transcripts, advisor support, and course materials.
- **Critical literacy analysis:** It explains how communication mediates power.
- **Design:** It proposes concrete changes that prevent similar harm.
- **Social justice bridge:** It uses personal experience to advocate for students with less power.

## Evidence Handling Rules

Use evidence strongly but carefully.

Preferred wording:

- "The email record shows..."
- "The transcript records..."
- "The advisor framed the situation as..."
- "The record supports the interpretation that..."
- "This suggests a communication-access design problem..."

Avoid:

- unsupported claims about intent;
- personal insults;
- treating one professor as the whole social justice issue;
- using long transcript blocks when paraphrase plus citation will work;
- blurring the difference between documented record, personal interpretation, peer perception, and recommendation.

The pain should not be erased, but it should be organized into evidence-based analysis.

## Drafting Logic

The draft should move in this order:

### 1. Opening: The Bridge

Start with the idea that literacy can be the difference between being understood and being misread. Introduce the experience as a case of communication access injustice, then bridge outward to younger students and children.

### 2. Social Justice Issue

Define the issue as communication access injustice for neurodivergent and chronically ill students in STEM education.

Explain that the issue is historically and locally relevant because schools have long treated one kind of learner, communicator, language user, body, pace, and memory system as the default.

### 3. Evidence Case

Use selected evidence, organized by literacy type:

- **Syllabus literacy:** written vs. verbal requirements, hidden rules, grading ambiguity.
- **Accommodation literacy:** DSO, Flex Plan, health disclosures, unclear access pathways.
- **Scientific communication literacy:** code, documentation, reference genome instructions, formats, AI notes, flat files, Google Docs.
- **Team communication literacy:** Slack, peer evaluation, group conflict, instructor-created expectations.
- **Institutional literacy:** advising, Ombuds, withdrawal decisions, formal escalation.

### 4. Critical Analysis

Analyze how language, tone, timing, hidden rules, institutional jargon, and assumed audience literacy affected access.

Questions to answer:

- Who was the communication designed for?
- Who had to work hardest to decode it?
- What counted as valid evidence of understanding?
- Which expectations were written, verbal, implied, or changing?
- How did communication become a gatekeeping system?

### 5. Present-Day Initiative / Artifact

Connect the issue to a current campaign, article, or initiative. Possible options:

- Universal Design for Learning (UDL)
- inclusive course design
- disability-access communication
- plain-language academic communication
- neurodiversity-affirming higher education communication

The final submission must include images or screenshots and analyze:

- target audience;
- expected literacy levels;
- accessibility strategies;
- remaining communication barriers.

### 6. Recommendations

Recommendations should be practical, specific, and grounded in the evidence.

Possible recommendations:

1. Put all requirements in one stable written location.
2. Use plain-language assignment summaries.
3. Clearly label which communication channel controls deadlines and requirements.
4. Avoid verbal-only changes for graded expectations.
5. Treat accommodations as the floor, not the full design.
6. Provide multimodal ways to demonstrate thinking.
7. Keep disability-related corrections private.
8. Use structured check-ins to clarify requirements.
9. Build explicit peer-team communication norms.
10. Train STEM faculty in neurodivergent communication, chronic illness variability, and accessible rigor.

### 7. Critical Reflection: From Pain to Protection

End by returning to the deeper purpose. Piter's documented experience should become a bridge to social justice, especially for students who are younger, less powerful, less resourced, or unable to advocate.

The reflection should name growth without sanitizing the harm:

- documentation became survival;
- literacy became advocacy;
- pain became evidence;
- evidence became design;
- design became protection for future students.

## Tone Standards

The final journal should be:

- emotionally truthful;
- academically organized;
- evidence-grounded;
- justice-oriented;
- protective of future students;
- clear about the difference between fact, perception, and interpretation;
- connected to Puzzle Plan and EQUITAS.

It should not sound like a detached paper. It should sound like a person using documented experience to build something better.

## Puzzle Plan / EQUITAS Role

Puzzle Plan lens:

- **Access:** communication must be readable, stable, findable, and multimodal.
- **Identity:** neurodivergence, chronic illness, CLD identity, accent, age, and technical expertise shape how students are perceived.
- **Healing:** the journal turns misrecognition into protective design.
- **Agency:** evidence becomes advocacy.
- **Criticality:** the paper questions default learners, hidden rules, and institutional language power.
- **Joy:** future students deserve learning without fear, shame, and constant self-defense.
- **Transformation:** the goal is a better communication model, not revenge.

EQUITAS guiding question:

> Does the communication design give students real access, dignity, and multiple ways to demonstrate knowledge, or does it quietly sort students by how well they survive ambiguity?
