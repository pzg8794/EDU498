# Assignment Requirements and Working Approach

## Assignment Title

**Literacy as Power: Bridging Social Justice and Critical Communication**

## Submission Window

**June 14 through June 21**

## Assignment Description

This assignment asks for a critical exploration of the relationship between literacy and social justice. The project must show how literacy can either empower or exclude communities. The student chooses a relevant social justice issue, analyzes how that issue is communicated, attends to language/accessibility/impact across audiences, connects the issue to a present-day campaign/article/initiative, and proposes more inclusive communication strategies.

The assignment allows flexible format choices such as paper, presentation, podcast, or other organized format.

## Objective

Engage in critical analysis and examine the role literacy plays in communicating social justice issues, with emphasis on how literacy can either empower or exclude communities.

## Required Components

### 1. Research and Documentation

- Identify and define a historically relevant social justice topic in a field of study or local community.
- Explain why the issue is important to draw attention to.
- Collect relevant sources such as articles, reports, community narratives, online discussions, emails, transcripts, and institutional records.
- Cite sources appropriately.
- Pay attention to language, tone, and terminology used in the sources.

### 2. Critical Analysis

- Investigate and detail how literacy influences the way the issue is communicated to the public or to affected communities.
- Examine whether specific jargon, terminology, hidden rules, or institutional language excludes groups from understanding or engaging with the issue.
- Consider how the literacy levels and access needs of different audiences affect communication.

### 3. Connect Issue to the Present

Connect the selected issue to a present-day social media campaign, article, or initiative. Include images or screenshots.

Analyze:

- target audience and expected literacy levels;
- strategies used to make information accessible and understandable;
- potential communication barriers caused by literacy challenges;
- how the artifact helps support the reflection.

### 4. Recommendations

Propose strategies to address an existing communication gap related to the chosen issue. Strategies should be inclusive and accessible across literacy levels. Possible options include multimedia, plain language, multimodal communication, community-based dialogue, clearer institutional procedures, and accessible documentation.

### 5. Critical Reflection

Reflect on findings by synthesizing:

- the issue;
- research and documentation;
- critical analysis;
- present-day campaign/article/initiative;
- recommendations;
- personal experience or observation;
- the role of literacy in social change.

## Chosen Social Justice Issue

**Communication access injustice for neurodivergent and chronically ill students in STEM education.**

This issue matters because course communication determines who is read as capable, prepared, responsible, respectful, and worthy of support. When a course relies on vague requirements, verbal-only changes, hidden grading procedures, single-mode evidence, public correction, unclear accommodation pathways, and rigid expectations, literacy becomes a gatekeeping system.

This is especially harmful for students with ADHD, chronic illness, trauma histories, multilingual/CLD identities, or other access needs. The problem is not only whether an accommodation exists on paper. The deeper issue is whether the communication design makes participation possible.

## Working Thesis Direction

Literacy is power in STEM classrooms because communication determines access. When requirements are vague, verbal-only, inconsistent, or tied to one assumed way of thinking and communicating, neurodivergent and chronically ill students can be misread as careless, difficult, unprepared, or less capable. Using a documented BIO/STEM course conflict as evidence, this journal argues that inclusive communication is not a kindness added to rigor; it is part of rigor. To bridge social justice and critical communication, educators must design course language, team structures, accommodation processes, and assessment options so students can access learning without sacrificing health, dignity, or identity.

## Core Approach: The Bridge

This journal should not read as a simple grievance narrative. It should read as a bridge from lived harm to social change.

The emotional center is:

> If this happened to an adult neurodivergent, chronically ill graduate student with documentation habits, technical expertise, advisor support, and the ability to advocate, what happens to children and younger students who cannot defend themselves yet?

The purpose is to use the pain as evidence, not as spectacle. The paper should show:

1. what happened;
2. where the record documents it;
3. how communication design created or worsened access barriers;
4. why this pattern matters for neurodivergent and chronically ill students;
5. why children and less-powerful students are even more vulnerable;
6. what inclusive communication changes could prevent similar harm.

## Evidence Handling Rules

Use evidence carefully and powerfully.

Preferred language:

- "The email record shows..."
- "The transcript records a student reporting..."
- "The advisor framed the concern as..."
- "The evidence suggests a communication-access design problem..."
- "The record supports the interpretation that..."

Avoid:

- personal insults;
- claims about intent unless the record directly supports them;
- naming the professor in the final journal unless needed and approved;
- overclaiming beyond the evidence;
- turning the professor into the whole story.

The professor/course can appear as the evidence site, but the social justice issue is broader: inaccessible communication systems that make neurodivergent and chronically ill students fight to be understood.

## Recommended Journal Structure

1. **Opening / The Bridge**
   - Start with the moral center: literacy as the difference between being understood and being misread.
   - Introduce the child/future-student concern.

2. **The Social Justice Issue**
   - Define communication access injustice in STEM education.
   - Explain why it matters historically and locally.

3. **Evidence Case: When Communication Becomes Exclusion**
   - Use selected evidence from emails/transcripts/advisor support.
   - Organize by literacy type: syllabus literacy, accommodation literacy, scientific communication literacy, institutional literacy, team communication literacy.

4. **Critical Literacy Analysis**
   - Analyze language, tone, hidden rules, verbal-only expectations, jargon, and audience assumptions.
   - Connect to Module 3/4 literacy frameworks.

5. **Present-Day Initiative / Artifact**
   - Connect to UDL, inclusive course design, disability-access communication, or plain-language academic communication.
   - Include screenshot(s).
   - Analyze target audience, literacy levels, accessibility strategies, and barriers.

6. **Recommendations: Communication as Access Design**
   - Offer practical changes for STEM instructors and institutions.

7. **Critical Reflection: From Pain to Protection**
   - Reflect personally without losing the broader justice lens.
   - End by returning to students and children who cannot defend themselves yet.

## Puzzle Plan / EQUITAS Integration

This assignment is a natural Puzzle Plan piece because it connects:

- **Access:** communication must be readable, findable, multimodal, and consistent.
- **Identity:** neurodivergence, chronic illness, CLD identity, accent, age, and technical expertise shape how students are perceived.
- **Healing:** the paper turns misrecognition and pain into protective design.
- **Agency:** documentation becomes a way to reclaim control and advocate for change.
- **Criticality:** the paper questions hidden rules, default learners, and institutional language power.
- **Joy:** future students deserve classrooms where they can learn without fear, shame, or constant self-defense.
- **Transformation:** the goal is not revenge; it is a communication model that prevents harm.

EQUITAS asks the guiding question:

> Does the communication design give students real access, dignity, and multiple ways to demonstrate knowledge, or does it quietly sort students by how well they survive ambiguity?
