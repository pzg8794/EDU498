## Statement of Truth / Opening Compass

The first thing readers should understand is this:

> This paper dives into the intersection of literacy and social justice by showing how communication systems can empower or exclude communities. In the BIO/STEM evidence case, course literacy did not only mean reading and writing. It meant being able to decode requirements, accommodations, grading language, verbal-only rules, team expectations, scientific communication norms, and institutional pathways. When a teacher uses their authority, knowledge, or influence to communicate through one-size-fits-all expectations, they can intentionally or unintentionally exclude neurodivergent, chronically ill, disabled, CLD, trauma-impacted, and otherwise vulnerable students.

This must be the first truth the paper establishes before moving into cases.

The professor/course evidence should not be used as gossip or revenge. It should be used as evidence of how power works through literacy. The paper's core claim is that communication design determines who is readable as capable, prepared, responsible, and worthy of support.

## 1. Research & Documentation

### Rubric Requirement

Identify and define a historically relevant social justice topic in the field of study or local community. Explain why the issue is important to draw attention to. Collect and cite relevant sources, including articles, reports, community narratives, online discussions, and other evidence. Pay attention to language, tone, and terminology.

### Our Topic

**Communication access injustice for neurodivergent and chronically ill students in STEM education.**

### Definition

Communication access injustice occurs when educational systems expect students to decode learning requirements, grading policies, accommodations, institutional procedures, and disciplinary norms through inaccessible communication structures. These structures may include vague language, verbal-only changes, hidden expectations, single-mode evidence, public correction, unclear accommodation procedures, and inconsistent communication channels.

### Why This Issue Matters

This issue matters because communication determines access. When students cannot access the language, structure, and expectations of a classroom, they cannot fully access the learning. For neurodivergent and chronically ill students, inaccessible communication can trigger health symptoms, executive-function collapse, shame, anxiety, relationship damage, academic misrecognition, and institutional exhaustion.

This issue is even more urgent for children and younger students. An adult graduate student may be able to document, email advisors, contact DSO, and identify patterns. A child often cannot. A child may simply be labeled lazy, disrespectful, careless, dramatic, inattentive, disruptive, or not ready.

## 2. Conduct a Critical Analysis

### Rubric Requirement

Investigate and detail how literacy influences how the issue is communicated to the public. Examine whether jargon or terminology excludes certain groups from understanding or engaging with the issue. Consider how different literacy levels impact communication effectiveness.

### Analysis Frame

The paper should treat literacy as a system of power across multiple levels:

- **Syllabus literacy:** Can students find and understand course requirements?
- **Policy literacy:** Can students understand how grading, submissions, late work, feedback, and exceptions operate?
- **Accommodation literacy:** Can students understand and use DSO/Flex Plan processes without repeated private disclosure?
- **Institutional literacy:** Can students identify when to contact advisors, Ombuds, DSO, or formal escalation channels?
- **Scientific communication literacy:** Can students communicate through code, documentation, data, reports, diagrams, visualizations, and disciplinary tools?
- **Team communication literacy:** Can students understand how Slack, Google Docs, meetings, peer evaluations, and task trackers are supposed to function?
- **Critical literacy:** Can students recognize when a communication pattern is not merely confusing, but exclusionary?

### How Literacy Influences Public Communication of the Issue

The paper should explain that accessibility issues are often communicated publicly through institutional language such as:

- accommodations;
- compliance;
- documentation;
- Flex Plans;
- reasonable adjustments;
- learning outcomes;
- rigor;
- professionalism;
- participation;
- student responsibility.

These terms may sound neutral, but they can hide the lived reality of students who must fight through unclear systems. The public language often frames access as an individual support process instead of a classroom communication design issue.

The critical analysis should argue that the BIO/STEM evidence reveals a bigger pattern: when communication is unclear, the burden shifts to the student. Students must prove they misunderstood for legitimate reasons, prove their disability matters, prove their documentation is valid, and prove they are not being careless.

### Jargon / Terminology That Can Exclude

The paper should identify terms that can exclude students if not explained clearly:

- "Flex Plan"
- "DSO"
- "Ombuds"
- "formal escalation"
- "10 out of 12"
- "first ten submissions"
- "formative feedback"
- "myCourses Assignment Box"
- "peer evaluation"
- "handwritten requirement"
- "flat file"
- "reference genome"
- "RNA-seq"
- "NovaSeq"
- "differential expression"
- "rubric"
- "feedback mechanisms"
- "professional communication"
- "rigor"

The goal is not to remove all specialized language. The goal is to teach and communicate it accessibly. Specialized language becomes exclusionary when it is assumed, hidden, shifting, or used without clear written support.

### Different Audience Literacy Levels

The analysis must consider multiple audiences:

1. **Graduate students with technical expertise but access needs**
   - May understand the science but struggle with ambiguous institutional language.

2. **Undergraduate students in group projects**
   - May misunderstand roles, technical expectations, communication norms, and conflict expectations.

3. **Neurodivergent students**
   - May rely on written records, transcripts, calendars, explicit expectations, and predictable communication.

4. **Chronically ill students**
   - May need flexible participation structures and reduced disclosure burden.

5. **CLD / multilingual students**
   - May need explicit explanations of institutional terms, grading policies, and unwritten norms.

6. **Students with trauma, depression, or anxiety**
   - May experience public correction, ambiguity, or conflict as threat, shutdown, or avoidance.

7. **Children and K-12 students**
   - Often cannot name the system, advocate through email, or seek formal help. They need teachers to design communication access before harm happens.

8. **Teachers and professors**
   - May have disciplinary expertise but limited training in disability-inclusive communication.

9. **Advisors/support staff**
   - Help translate confusion into institutional options. Their communication can empower students when classroom communication has excluded them.

## 3. Connect Issue to the Present

### Rubric Requirement

Connect the issue to a present-day social media campaign, article, or initiative. Include images/screenshots. Analyze target audience, literacy levels, accessibility strategies, and potential communication barriers caused by literacy challenges. All of this must be included in the reflection.

### Present-Day Initiative

Primary initiative:

**Universal Design for Learning (UDL), especially CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0.**

UDL works because the evidence is not asking for lower expectations. It is asking for course design that makes rigorous learning accessible from the beginning.

### Why UDL Is the Right Bridge

UDL gives language for:

- multiple means of engagement;
- multiple means of representation;
- multiple means of action and expression;
- accessible communication;
- learner agency;
- welcoming identities;
- clarifying language and symbols;
- using multiple media;
- organizing information;
- reducing exclusionary barriers.

### Teaching Placement Bridge

The present-day connection should not stop at UDL. It should also connect to Piter's teaching placement, because the placement shows the K-12 version of the issue.

The BIO/STEM evidence shows what happens to an adult when communication access fails. The teaching placement helps show why the same issue is even more urgent for children. Children need instructions, visuals, repetition, modeling, peer support, multimodal access, and emotionally safe clarification. They often show understanding through movement, building, drawing, coding, talking, collaborating, trial-and-error, and peer teaching.

This strengthens the reflection because it shows growth: Piter is not only processing personal harm; he is using the harm to notice what children need before they are harmed.

### Target Audiences and Expected Literacy Levels

The paper should name these audiences:

- neurodivergent students;
- chronically ill students;
- disabled students;
- students with childhood trauma;
- students with depression/anxiety;
- CLD and multilingual students;
- children/K-12 learners;
- undergraduates navigating STEM teams;
- graduate students navigating institutional systems;
- teachers/professors who may not know how inaccessible their communication is;
- advisors and support staff who help students navigate harm.

Expected literacy levels vary by audience. The paper should not treat literacy as a simple high/low scale. Instead, it should explain that different audiences may have different kinds of literacy: scientific, institutional, digital, emotional, disability, policy, language, and self-advocacy literacy.

### Strategies Used by UDL / Inclusive Communication

Strategies to analyze:

- using multiple modes of representation;
- clarifying vocabulary, symbols, expectations, and processes;
- providing multiple means of action and expression;
- supporting self-regulation and executive function;
- welcoming identities and reducing threat;
- making information findable and stable;
- designing for learner variability rather than treating difference as exception.

### Potential Communication Barriers

Even UDL can fail if communicated poorly. Barriers include:

- professional jargon;
- long framework documents that are hard for busy teachers to translate into practice;
- treating UDL as optional kindness instead of design responsibility;
- focusing on disability compliance instead of everyday communication access;
- not translating UDL into concrete practices for syllabi, emails, grading policies, team projects, and feedback;
- failing to connect adult higher-education access issues with K-12 student vulnerability.

## Recommendations Alignment

The recommendations section must directly answer the assignment requirement: propose strategies to address the communication gap.

### Puzzle Plan as Strategy Engine

The Puzzle Plan should organize the recommendations:

- **Access:** stable written requirements, plain language, multimodal instructions.
- **Identity:** protect neurodivergent, chronically ill, CLD, trauma-impacted, and disabled students from being misread.
- **Healing:** reduce shame, public correction, and repeated disclosure burden.
- **Agency:** give students clear pathways to ask questions, clarify expectations, and choose expression modes.
- **Criticality:** teach students and teachers to recognize when communication design reproduces exclusion.
- **Joy:** make learning possible without fear and constant self-defense.
- **Transformation:** turn painful evidence into a redesigned communication model.

### EQUITAS as Accessibility Standard

EQUITAS should be the framework for equivalent access across literacy levels.

EQUITAS in this paper means:

> Students do not all need identical communication. They need communication that gives them an equivalent opportunity to understand, participate, and demonstrate knowledge with dignity.

Possible EQUITAS-aligned strategies:

1. All graded expectations must be written, findable, and stable.
2. Verbal updates must be repeated in writing.
3. Every assignment should include a plain-language summary.
4. Course communication should name which channel controls policy, deadlines, and feedback.
5. Students should have multimodal ways to demonstrate thinking when the mode is not the learning target.
6. Disability-related clarification should happen privately.
7. Group projects should include explicit communication norms.
8. Faculty should be trained in neurodivergent communication and chronic illness variability.
9. Support systems should be explained in student-facing language before crisis.
10. Every communication policy should be tested with the question: would this work for a child who cannot advocate like an adult?

### Multimedia / Simplified Language / Community Dialogue

Use these as practical recommendations:

- visual assignment maps;
- one-page plain-language syllabus guides;
- short video walkthroughs of major assignments;
- checklists for group projects;
- accessible calendars/deadline trackers;
- examples/non-examples of submissions;
- private clarification forms;
- community dialogue norms for team conflict;
- peer-support structures that do not place all responsibility on the most technically skilled or most vulnerable student.
